Chapter 3 — Physical Examination of the Infant and Child

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Part I — Foundations of Pediatric Practical Care, Chapter 3

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Prepare the examination environment (lighting, warmth, privacy, play) and gather the required equipment.
  2. Approach the child according to developmental age and stage (infant, toddler, preschooler, school-age, adolescent).
  3. Apply the Nursing Care Guidelines for performing a pediatric physical examination (nonthreatening order, secure position, ABC in emergencies).
  4. Use the four assessment techniques (inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation) in the correct order for each system.
  5. Sequence the examination head-to-toe with painful/intrusive areas last.
  6. Read the child's readiness signals and apply the refusal techniques.
  7. Perform the lab and hospital steps of a systematic head-to-toe examination using the checklist.

2. Placement Box

Ward: All wards. Each ministerial chapter re-trains a focused examination:

NICU — newborn examination (jaundice: skin/sclerae; RDS: respiratory);

Blood ward — lymph nodes, liver/spleen, joints;

Medical ward — chest auscultation (pneumonia), skin turgor/capillary refill (dehydration);

Surgical ward — abdominal examination (Hirschsprung, pyloric stenosis).

4. Preparing the Physical Examination

Being prepared and organized ensures efficiency; the approach depends on the child's developmental stage.

Environment and equipment:

  • Room well lit and decorated with neutral colors; temperature comfortably warm.
  • Strange, potentially frightening equipment kept out of sight; bring out one piece at a time.
  • Toys/games available; washable toys as distractions.
  • Privacy, especially for school-age children and adolescents.
  • Provide time for play and becoming acquainted.

Gather the equipment: clean gloves, stethoscope, thermometer, sphygmomanometer, tape measure, reflex hammer, penlight, otoscope/ophthalmoscope, tongue depressor, cotton ball, infant or adult scale, stadiometer.

  • Display a confident, matter-of-fact approach; frustration is sensed by child and parent.
  • Keep the room warm; allow the child to remain dressed until the time of the examination.

5. Approaching the Child by Developmental Stage

General rule: approach the child according to developmental age; if several children are seen, begin with the most cooperative child to model the behavior.

Newborns and infants:

  • If the infant is asleep, auscultate the heart, lungs, and abdomen first while the baby is quiet; count HR and RR before undressing.
  • Completely undress down to the diaper; remove the diaper last (genitalia, anus, spine, hips).
  • Examine 1–2 hours before a feeding; have the parent hold the child; parent stays the nurturer, not an assistant of painful procedures.
  • Head-to-toe order; most traumatic (ears, nose, mouth, throat) last; delay the Moro reflex until the end (startle → crying).
  • Warm hands and stethoscope; soft crooning voice, smile, eye contact; bright objects; pacifier if crying.
  • Stranger anxiety is normal in older infants — keep the parent in view.

Toddlers:

  • Remove clothing one item at a time; replace clothing after each area; gown not needed before school age.
  • Use little touch at the beginning; introduce equipment slowly; let the child touch it, take a parent's temperature, put the cuff on a teddy bear.
  • Sit on the caregiver's lap; knee-to-knee for the abdominal examination.
  • Praise cooperation.
  • Use short phrases telling what you'll do, not asking permission. Toddlers are egocentric — don't reference another child's behavior.

Preschoolers:

  • May fear body invasion/mutilation; withdraw from intrusive steps; use simple explanations and reassurance.
  • Let the child "help" (hold stethoscope/penlight); offer real choices; compliment cooperation.
  • Games encourage deep breathing: hold up a finger or penlight and "blow it out".

School-age:

  • Concrete thinking — avoid jargon and double-meaning words: "Let's see how warm you are" instead of "take your temperature".
  • Respect body privacy: keep underpants on under the gown; replace clothing promptly.
  • Commenting on findings is interesting to them — they like to learn how the body works.

Adolescents:

  • Provide privacy while undressing; expose only the area examined; matter-of-fact comments about physical changes; allow questions without the caregiver present; no "dumb questions".

6. General Rules: Performing the Pediatric Examination

Nursing Care Guidelines — Performing Pediatric Physical Examination:

  • Observe readiness signals: talking to the nurse, eye contact, accepting equipment, allowing touch, choosing the examining table over the parent's lap.
  • If not ready: talk to the parent while "ignoring" the child, making complimentary remarks; tell a funny story or do a magic trick; use a hand puppet "friend".
  • If the child refuses: assess the reason (an unduly afraid child may have had a traumatic experience); involve child and parent; avoid prolonged explanations; use a firm, direct approach; perform quickly; gentle restraint by an attendant; minimize stimulation; quiet calm confident voice.
  • Begin nonthreateningly: games (cranial-nerve tests, Simon Says); paper-doll technique — trace the child's outline and use it to demonstrate what will be examined.
  • Involve the child: choices (table vs lap), allow handling equipment, demonstrate on a doll or parent.
  • Sequence: usually head-to-toe with exceptions: alter for age (Table 4.2); examine painful areas last; in an emergency examine ABC (airway, breathing, circulation) first.
  • Reassure throughout (especially pubertal body concerns); discuss findings with the family at the end; praise and reward the child.

7. Assessment Techniques (IPPA)

The four techniques are used in system-specific order. For most systems the order is inspection → palpation → percussion → auscultation; for the abdomen the order is inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation — palpation last because it may alter bowel sounds.

  • Inspection: observation first (color, shape, movement, symmetry). Adequate lighting is necessary.
  • Palpation: superficial then deep; light for most areas; to reduce tickling, place the child's hand over the palpating hand or have the child squeeze the parent's hand.
  • Percussion: tone assessment (resonance/dullness) in chest and abdomen.
  • Auscultation: use the appropriate side of the stethoscope (bell = low frequency e.g., heart sounds; diaphragm = high frequency e.g., breath sounds); in infants auscultate before palpation/percussion of the chest and do it when the infant is quiet.

8. Sequence of the Examination (Head-to-Toe Model)

The examination is recorded following the head-to-toe model even when the actual order is altered for the child.

  1. Vital functions first in an emergency (ABC).
  2. Growth measurements — weight, length/height, head circumference, arm circumference (see Chapter 4).
  3. General appearance — alertness, posture, hygiene, interaction.
  4. Head and neck — fontanelles, suture lines, eyes (red reflex), ears, nose, throat (last, most traumatic), neck lymph nodes.
  5. Chest — inspection (shape, retractions), auscultation of breath and heart sounds (quiet first).
  6. Abdomen — inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation (superficial first).
  7. Genitalia, anus, spine, hips — last; in infants after undressing fully.
  8. Neurologic — reflexes (Moro delayed to the end), motor tone, cranial nerves.

Record observations, not only the abnormal — normal findings are baseline data for the ward notes.

9. Nursing Care Plan (Examination/Preparation Focus)

Assessment: preschooler refuses the examination, hides behind mother, responds to the stethoscope by crying.

NANDA diagnosis: Anxiety (child) related to perceived body invasion/mutilation.

Goals: The child will cooperate with a modified examination; verbal/nonverbal distress will decrease.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Let the child "help" with the stethoscope/penlight and offer choices (lap vs table) — restores a sense of control.
  2. Use the paper-doll technique to demonstrate steps — reduces fear of the unknown.
  3. Explain each step in simple, concrete language ("I want to listen to you breathe") — matches concrete thinking.
  4. Use a game to elicit deep breathing ("blow out the light") — gains cooperation without force.
  5. Preserve privacy (underpants under the gown) and praise cooperation — supports body integrity and positive reinforcement.
  6. Evaluate: the child allows auscultation/palpation; crying stops; parent reports reduced fear.

11. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Repeat the checklist at the bedside with the real child and family.
  • Verify the child's readiness; if refusal occurs, use the refusal techniques rather than forcing.
  • Record normal and abnormal findings; report abnormal findings to the physician.
  • Always document the parent's presence and the child's cooperation level.

12. Case Study (Examination)

Ali, 4 years. Admitted for pneumonia. On approach, he hides and refuses any touching. The nurse uses the paper-doll technique, a game ("let's see how warm you are"), and lets him hold the stethoscope on his teddy bear first. He then allows chest auscultation, where crackles are heard at the right base. Temperature 38.9°C.

Critical-thinking questions:

  1. Why was chest auscultation deferred until rapport was established? (Sequence flexibility — painful/intrusive last; readiness signals honored.)
  2. How does "let's see how warm you are" help? (Avoids medical jargon with double meaning; concrete language for the preschooler.)
  3. Which examination order rule was applied when the nurse examined the chest before the throat? (Most traumatic areas last.)
  4. What should be documented besides the abnormal finding? (Normal baseline findings, cooperation level, parent presence.)

14. Key Points

  • Prepare the environment (light, warmth, privacy, equipment out of sight) and approach by developmental stage.
  • ABC first in emergencies; painful/intrusive areas last; Moro reflex last in infants.
  • Auscultate the quiet infant first (heart, lungs, abdomen).
  • Use IPPA with the abdomen order: inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation.
  • Apply the readiness/refusal techniques and the paper-doll technique.
  • Record findings in the head-to-toe model regardless of exam order.
