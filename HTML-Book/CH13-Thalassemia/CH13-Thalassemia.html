1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain the genetics (autosomal recessive, 25% risk per pregnancy) and pathophysiology of beta thalassemia major.
  2. Recognize the clinical manifestations: anemia, pallor, fever, hepatosplenomegaly, bone changes, and growth failure; and the classic hematologic picture (microcytic/hypochromic RBCs, target cells; Hb electrophoresis for diagnosis).
  3. Describe the medical management: transfusion therapy (target Hgb ≈ 9.5 g/dL), iron chelation (deferoxamine, deferasirox, deferiprone), splenectomy, immunization, and cure by HSCT.
  4. Implement nursing care: transfusion monitoring, chelation compliance education, prevention of postsplenectomy sepsis, and family/genetic counseling.

2. Placement Box

Ward: Blood ward — one of the three hematology topics. Thalassemia is the classic chronic hemolytic anemia requiring repeated transfusion + chelation.

4. Definition and Genetics

  • Thalassemia is the term for inherited blood disorders with a deficiency in the rate of production of specific globin chains in hemoglobin; the name derives from the Greek thalassa ("sea"), referring to Mediterranean populations.
  • Beta thalassemia is the most common form, classified as:
  • Thalassemia minor/trait — heterozygous, usually mild microcytic anemia
  • Thalassemia intermedia — moderate–severe anemia with splenomegaly
  • Thalassemia major (Cooley anemia) — homozygous, severe anemia not compatible with life without transfusion support.
  • Mode of transmission: autosomal recessive; when both parents carry the trait, each pregnancy carries a 25% chance of a child with thalassemia major.

5. Pathophysiology

  • Normal HbA is composed of two α and two β polypeptide chains. In beta thalassemia there is a partial or complete deficiency in β-chain synthesis, with compensatory excess of α-chains and continued γ-chain production → unstable, defective hemoglobin.
  • The unstable polypeptide disintegrates, damaging RBCs and causing severe hemolytic anemia; the bone marrow overproduces erythrocytes unless suppressed by transfusions.
  • Excess iron from transfusions and rapid destruction of defective cells is stored in organs (hemosiderosis).

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Before diagnosis: anemia, pallor, unexplained fever, poor feeding, enlarged spleen or liver.
  • Progressive anemia: signs of chronic hypoxia, headache, bone pain, decreased exercise tolerance, listlessness, anorexia.
  • Other features: small stature, delayed sexual maturation, bronzed/freckled complexion (without chelation).
  • Bone changes (untreated, older children): enlarged head, frontal/parietal bossing, prominent malar eminences, flat nasal bridge, enlarged maxilla, protruding upper central incisors/malocclusion, generalized osteoporosis.
  • Onset in major may be insidious, recognized in late infancy or early toddlerhood.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • CBC/smear: microcytosis, hypochromia, anisocytosis, poikilocytosis, target cells, basophilic stippling; low Hgb and Hct in severe anemia.
  • Hemoglobin electrophoresis confirms the diagnosis and distinguishes the type by analyzing the quantity/kind of Hb variants.
  • Screen the whole family for the trait; refer for genetic counseling; prenatal diagnosis by amniocentesis or fetal blood sampling is available.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • Transfusions are the foundation of management; goal: maintain Hgb at approximately 9.5 g/dL, possibly requiring transfusion every 2–5 weeks. Benefits: normal activity, decreased cardiomegaly/hepatosplenomegaly, fewer bone changes, near-normal growth, fewer infections.
  • Iron chelation for hemosiderosis/hemochromatosis (the body cannot eliminate excess iron): deferoxamine (parenteral/subcutaneous infusions), deferasirox (oral), and deferiprone (oral).
  • Splenectomy may be needed for severe splenomegaly/increased transfusion requirements; major complication = severe overwhelming infection — children usually receive prophylactic antibiotics for years plus pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccines.
  • NURSING TIP: the family must notify health personnel of any fever ≥38.5 °C (101.3 °F) because of sepsis risk from asplenia.
  • Cure: allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) — about 80–97% complication-free survival in children <14 years without excessive iron stores/hepatomegaly; gene-therapy approaches are under study.
  • Prognosis: with transfusions + early chelation, survival into adulthood; common causes of death are heart disease, liver cirrhosis, postsplenectomy sepsis, and multiorgan failure from hemochromatosis.

9. Nursing Care Plan (Chronic Care)

Assessment: 6-year-old with thalassemia major, Hgb 7.2 g/dL, attends every 3 weeks for transfusion; missed 2 chelation doses this month; parents concerned about injections.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Deficient knowledge (parents/child) related to chelation therapy.
  • Risk for injury (transfusion reaction / infection) related to repeated transfusions and asplenia.

Goals: Compliance with chelation; complication-free transfusions; parents identify the fever rule; Hgb maintained ≈9.5 g/dL.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Explain the defect, its effect on RBCs, and the consequences of untreated iron overload (delayed growth, heart disease) — motivates adherence.
  2. Teach and supervise the chelation regimen (deferoxamine infusions / oral agents); acknowledge that needle/infusion anxiety is common and support coping — improves adherence.
  3. Monitor every transfusion: baseline vitals, correct blood product, observation for reactions — prevents/early-manages reactions.
  4. Fever ≥38.5 °C = report immediately (asplenia sepsis risk); ensure pneumococcal/meningococcal/regular vaccines.
  5. Support chronic-illness adjustment and refer for genetic counseling (25% recurrence risk) — family coping and informed choices.
  6. Evaluate: Hgb maintained near 9.5 g/dL; no sepsis/transfusion reactions; chelation doses documented; family states the fever rule.

11. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • On admission of a child for transfusion: weigh, obtain CBC, verify the product, and use the transfusion checklist.
  • Reinforce chelation teaching and confirm the next chelation dose is given in the ward/pharmacy; document iron studies (ferritin) results and coordinate with the hematologist.
  • Provide the postsplenectomy/at-risk immunization record review; schedule pneumococcal/meningococcal vaccines if due.

12. Case Study (Fever in Thalassemia)

Scenario: A 7-year-old boy with thalassemia major (postsplenectomy 1 year ago) is brought to the emergency room with fever 39.1 °C, chills, and listlessness.

  1. Why is this an emergency? (Postsplenectomy + fever = risk of overwhelming sepsis; fever ≥38.5 °C must be reported immediately.)
  2. Which preventive measures should have been in place? (Prophylactic antibiotics, pneumococcal + meningococcal + regular vaccines.)
  3. What is the likely long-term therapy-related risk in this child? (Iron overload/hemochromatosis → heart and liver disease if chelation is incomplete.)
  4. Which single test confirms thalassemia? (Hemoglobin electrophoresis.)

14. Key Points

  • Beta thalassemia major (Cooley anemia) = homozygous, autosomal recessive, severe anemia requiring transfusion support.
  • Key clinical: pallor, fever, hepatosplenomegaly, bone deformities of untreated children; hematology: microcytic/hypochromic + target cells; diagnosis by Hb electrophoresis.
  • Medical care: transfusions to Hgb ≈ 9.5 g/dL (q2–5 wk), iron chelation (deferoxamine/deferasirox/deferiprone), splenectomy if needed, immunizations, and HSCT for cure.
  • Nursing: transfusion safety, chelation compliance, fever ≥38.5 °C = emergency (asplenia sepsis), genetic counseling (25% risk).
