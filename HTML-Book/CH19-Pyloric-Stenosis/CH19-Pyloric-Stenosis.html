Chapter 19 — Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis (HPS)

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain the pathology of HPS: circular pyloric muscle hypertrophy → elongation and narrowing of the pyloric canal → gastric outlet obstruction.
  2. Identify the classic presentation: nonbilious projectile vomiting beginning at 3–6 weeks, an avaricious hungry infant, and the olive-shaped mass in the epigastrium.
  3. Interpret the metabolic picture: hypochloremia, hypokalemia, hyponatremia, metabolic alkalosis, elevated BUN.
  4. Describe the management: preoperative rehydration/electrolyte correction (NPO, IV fluids, NG decompression), pyloromyotomy (Fredet–Ramstedt), and staged postoperative feeding.
  5. Provide nursing care: observation, fluid therapy and I&O monitoring, NG tube patency, parental reassurance, and post-op feeding/wound care.

4. Definition and Epidemiology

  • Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (HPS) occurs when the circumferential muscle of the pyloric sphincter thickens, elongating and narrowing the pyloric canal → gastric outlet obstruction with compensatory dilation, hypertrophy, and hyperperistalsis of the stomach.
  • Develops in the first few weeks of life, causing nonbilious vomiting after feeding; projectile vomiting may develop, and the infant is fussy and hungry after vomiting; untreated → dehydration, metabolic alkalosis, and failure to thrive.
  • Boys are affected 4–6 times more often than girls; more common in white infants; incidence 2–3.5 per 1,000 live births, with 30–40% first-born infants; etiology multifactorial.
  • HPS is not a congenital disorder; local innervation may be involved; usually an isolated lesion, but may associate with malrotation, esophageal/duodenal atresia, and anorectal anomalies.

5. Pathophysiology

  • The circular pyloric muscle thickens → severe narrowing of the pyloric canal between stomach and duodenum; the lumen is partially obstructed, and over time inflammation and edema further reduce the opening → complete obstruction.
  • The hypertrophied pylorus is palpable as an olive-like mass in the upper abdomen.

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Projectile vomiting: may be ejected 3–4 feet when side-lying, 1 foot or more when back-lying; usually shortly after a feeding; may follow each feeding or appear intermittently; nonbilious vomitus that may be blood tinged.
  • Infant hungry, avid nurser — eagerly accepts a second feeding after vomiting; no evidence of pain except chronic hunger; poor weight gain; signs of dehydration.
  • Distended upper abdomen; readily palpable olive-shaped tumor in the epigastrium just to the right of the umbilicus; visible gastric peristaltic waves moving left to right across the epigastrium.
  • Vomiting usually begins at 4 weeks of age (range 1 week to 5 months), occurring 10–30 minutes after feeding and becoming projectile as obstruction progresses.
  • Mild indirect hyperbilirubinemia (decreased glucuronyl transferase) may occur and resolves with surgical correction.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Diagnosis is often made after history and physical examination; the olive is most easily palpated when the stomach is empty, the infant is quiet, and abdominal muscles relaxed.
  • If inconclusive: ultrasonography demonstrates an elongated mass surrounding a long pyloric canal; if US is negative, upper GI radiography rules out other causes.
  • A pacifier or nipple dipped in glucose water may soothe the irritable infant enough to complete the abdominal examination.
  • Laboratory findings (dehydration): decreased serum sodium and potassium (may be masked by hemoconcentration); more diagnostically useful — decrease in serum chloride and increases in pH and bicarbonate (metabolic alkalosis); elevated BUN from dehydration.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • Surgical relief by pyloromyotomy is the standard therapy. Preoperatively the infant must be rehydrated and the metabolic alkalosis corrected with parenteral fluids and electrolytes; replacement therapy usually delays surgery 24–48 hours; stomach decompressed with an NG tube if vomiting continues; infants without imbalance go to surgery without delay.
  • Surgery (often laparoscopic): longitudinal incision through the circular muscle fibers down to, but not including, the submucosa — pyloromyotomy (Fredet–Ramstedt operation); high success rate.
  • Feedings: usually begun 4–6 hours postoperatively with small, frequent feedings of water or electrolyte solution; if retained, formula in small increments about 24 hours after surgery; gradually increase until full schedule (~48 hours).
  • Prognosis: excellent with early diagnosis; mortality 0–0.5%; small percentage develop GERD.

9. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 5-week-old male, 1-week history of projectile nonbilious vomiting after feeds, hungry between episodes, weight falling (birth 3.6 kg → now 3.4 kg), signs of dehydration.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Deficient fluid volume related to vomiting.
  • Imbalanced nutrition: less than body requirements related to gastric outlet obstruction.
  • Risk for aspiration / ineffective airway related to projectile vomiting.
  • Deficient knowledge (parents) related to the disorder and post-op care.

Goals: Rehydrated and alkalosis corrected preoperatively; successful pyloromyotomy; staged feeds tolerated; parents confident and reassured.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Preoperative care — restore hydration/electrolytes: NPO, IV fluids with glucose and electrolytes based on serum values; careful monitoring of IV fluids and strict I & O; accurate description of vomiting and stools — corrects alkalosis before surgery.
  2. Monitor electrolytes: vitals especially for fluid/electrolyte imbalance; metabolic alkalosis from loss of H⁺ and depletion of K, Na, Cl in gastric secretions; assess skin/mucous membranes.
  3. NG decompression: keep the tube patent and functioning; measure/record type and amount of drainage if used preoperatively.
  4. Assist in diagnosis: observe feeding behavior, peristaltic waves, and palpation of the olive (empty stomach, quiet, glucose-water pacifier).
  5. Reassure parents: the condition is a structural problem, not poor parenting; encourage visits and involvement in care — reduces anxiety.
  6. Postoperative feeding: start 12–24 h (water/electrolytes 4–6 h per protocol), small frequent amounts; give expressed breast milk or limit nursing time with gradual increase; record feedings and responses.
  7. Postoperative observation: expect some vomiting in the first 24–48 h; monitor vitals, IV fluids, I&O; give around-the-clock analgesics (pain is continuous); inspect incisions for drainage/erythema and report infection signs.
  8. Discharge teaching: incision/dressing care, feeding schedule and expectations, when to call the surgeon.
  9. Evaluate: urine output adequate, alkalosis corrected, feedings retained without projectile vomiting, parents verbalize confidence.

11. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • On admission: NPO, IV access, baseline electrolytes/BUN, weight, and abdominal exam; NG decompression if vomiting.
  • Confirm with ultrasound (or upper GI series); obtain surgical consent; correct alkalosis before pyloromyotomy.
  • Postoperation: staged feedings, analgesics, wound observation; discharge with feeding plan and incision care instructions.

12. Case Study (Projectile Vomiting)

Scenario: A 4-week-old boy began projectile, nonbilious vomiting 10–30 minutes after feeds, is always hungry, and now has a palpable olive felt just right of the umbilicus. Serum chloride 88 mEq/L, bicarbonate 34 mEq/L, K 3.2 mEq/L, BUN 24.

  1. Which diagnosis fits? (HPS — classic age, projectile nonbilious vomiting, hungry infant, olive mass.)
  2. Interpret the labs. (Hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis with elevated BUN from vomiting.)
  3. Why is surgery delayed? (To rehydrate and correct the alkalosis with parenteral fluids/electrolytes — ~24–48 h.)
  4. What operation is planned? (Pyloromyotomy — Fredet–Ramstedt; longitudinal division of the circular muscle to the submucosa; laparoscopic approach possible.)
  5. How is feeding resumed? (Small frequent water/electrolyte feeds 4–6 h post-op (12–24 h per protocol), then formula/breast milk in small increments, increased over ~48 h.)

14. Key Points

  • HPS = pyloric circular muscle hypertrophy → pyloric canal narrowing → gastric outlet obstruction; boys 4–6× girls; 2–3.5/1,000.
  • Classic: nonbilious projectile vomiting (3–6 weeks), hungry avid infant, olive mass in epigastrium, visible peristaltic waves; untreated → dehydration, hypochloremic hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, FTT.
  • Diagnosis: history + physical (empty stomach, glucose-water pacifier), ultrasound, upper GI series; labs: ↓Cl, ↓K, ↑pH/CO₂, ↑BUN.
  • Therapy: rehydrate/correct alkalosis (NPO, IV, NG) → pyloromyotomy; feedings start small and frequent 4–6 h post-op; prognosis excellent.
  • Nursing: strict I&O, electrolyte monitoring, NG patency, post-op analgesics/feeding/wound observation, parental reassurance (not a parenting failure).
