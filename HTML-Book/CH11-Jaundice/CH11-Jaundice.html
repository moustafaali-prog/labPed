Chapter 11 — Neonatal Jaundice (Hyperbilirubinemia)

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain the pathophysiology of unconjugated (physiologic) hyperbilirubinemia and distinguish physiologic, breastfeeding-associated, breast-milk, and hemolytic jaundice.
  2. Recognize pathologic warning signs: jaundice appearing within 24 hours, rapidly rising bilirubin (≥5 mg/dL/day), and a positive Coombs test.
  3. Provide evidence-based nursing care for the infant under phototherapy (eye protection, position, fluid/growth monitoring, temperature, skin care, charting).
  4. Prepare an infant and family for exchange transfusion and monitor for complications/transfusion reactions.
  5. Detect early signs of bilirubin encephalopathy (kernicterus) and report promptly.

2. Placement Box

Ward: NICU — one of the two neonatal topics. Jaundice is the most common newborn problem requiring ward admission, phototherapy, and (rarely) exchange transfusion.

Foundation skills reused: CH5 vital signs, CH6 neurologic/reflex assessment, CH7 fluid calculations, CH8 medication safety, CH10 core ward skills.

4. Definition and Pathophysiology

  • Hyperbilirubinemia is an excessive level of accumulated bilirubin in the blood, characterized by jaundice (icterus) — a yellowish discoloration of the skin and other organs.
  • Bilirubin is a breakdown product of hemoglobin from RBC destruction. When RBCs are destroyed, hemoglobin splits into heme and globin; the heme portion is converted to unconjugated bilirubin (insoluble, bound to albumin).
  • In the liver, bilirubin is detached from albumin and conjugated with glucuronic acid (enzyme: glucuronyl transferase) to a water-soluble product that is excreted in bile; bacteria reduce it to urobilinogen, the pigment giving stool its color.
  • When developmental limitations or disease interfere with this balance, bilirubin accumulates in the tissues → jaundice.
  • Causes: physiologic (prematurity), breastfeeding/breast-milk association, excess production (hemolysis, bruises), disturbed liver conjugation/excretion, certain diseases (G6PD deficiency, hypothyroidism, galactosemia, infant of diabetic mother), and genetic predisposition.

6. Assessment and Diagnosis

  • Visual assessment is NOT reliable to judge severity — jaundice is estimated by transcutaneous bilirubinometry (TcB) or total serum bilirubin (TSB); visual/transcutaneous estimates are invalid once phototherapy begins.
  • Risk factors for pathologic hyperbilirubinemia include jaundice with onset within 24 hours, rapidly rising levels, maternal blood group O, positive Coombs test, previous sibling with hyperbilirubinemia, and prematurity/illness.
  • Use the hour-specific bilirubin nomogram to determine the risk zone and the phototherapy/exchange thresholds for infants ≥35 weeks.
  • ABO incompatibility: treat with early detection + phototherapy; diagnosis may be hard because the direct Coombs test can be negative or weakly reactive; consider when jaundice appears within 24 hours, bilirubin rises, with RBC spherocytosis.

7. Complications: Bilirubin Encephalopathy and Kernicterus

  • Unconjugated bilirubin is highly toxic to neurons; severe hyperbilirubinemia risks bilirubin encephalopathy (early, varying, reversible symptoms) and kernicterus — yellow staining and necrosis of brain cells with chronic permanent damage.
  • Early signs: lethargy, poor feeding, hypotonia, high-pitched cry, temperature instability (some preterm infants may be asymptomatic).
  • Later signs: athetoid cerebral palsy, extrapyramidal symptoms, opisthotonos, dental enamel hyperplasia, motor delay, seizures, and sensorineural hearing loss — long-term cognitive, motor, and behavioral effects.
  • Safe practice: serum bilirubin alone does not predict CNS injury; risk and neurologic signs guide action.

8. Medical and Nursing Management

8.1 Phototherapy

  • Place the infant under the light source exposing as much skin as possible; position and reposition frequently to expose all body surfaces.
  • Monitor serum bilirubin every 6–12 hours after initiation (visual/TcB no longer valid).
  • Eye protection: opaque mask sized correctly, covering eyes completely without occluding the nares; close the eyelids before applying (corneal excoriation); check eyes every 4–6 hours for discharge/pressure/irritation; remove shields during feeding for visual/sensory stimulation.
  • Temperature: monitor for temperature instability — phototherapy may raise body temperature; maintain manufacturer-specified lamp distance (halogen lights too close cause burns); keep the infant flexed with rolled blankets.
  • Side effects: loose greenish stools (accelerated bilirubin excretion), transient rashes, mild hyperthermia, increased metabolic rate, priapism, rarely dehydration/hypocalcemia; meticulous perianal skin care; add fluids/feedings to compensate for insensible and stool losses; do not use oily lotions (increased tanning).
  • Bronze-baby syndrome: grayish-brown skin/serum/urine, usually with elevated conjugated bilirubin + cholestasis; resolves after discontinuing phototherapy.
  • Rebound effect: a transient rise in bilirubin after phototherapy stops — usually resolves without resuming therapy.
  • Charting must include: start/stop times, eye shielding, phototherapy unit type, number of lamps, lamp-to-infant distance, incubator vs open bassinet use, light intensity (µW), and observed side effects.
  • Family support: reassure parents the infant is warm and comfortable; remove shields and turn off lights during visits to facilitate attachment and eye contact.

8.2 Exchange Transfusion

  • Indications: rapidly rising bilirubin and hemolysis despite aggressive phototherapy; also hydrops fetalis or signs of cardiac failure (immediate procedure).
  • Procedure: sterile surgical technique; catheter inserted into the umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava; 5–10 mL blood withdrawn over 15–20 s and an equal volume of donor blood infused; donor blood typed/cross-matched to the mother's serum; usually double the infant's blood volume (~85 mL/kg), replacing ~85% of circulating blood.
  • Nursing during the procedure: infant stays NPO with a peripheral dextrose/electrolyte infusion; document volumes withdrawn/infused, times, and cumulative total; monitor vital signs continuously, correlate with blood removal/infusion; stop temporarily for cardiac/respiratory problems and resume when stable; observe for transfusion reaction (temperature instability, hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, rash); maintain thermoregulation, glucose, and fluid balance.
  • ABO incompatibility rarely needs exchange transfusion unless phototherapy fails.
  • Family support: parents often feel guilty about "causing" the incompatibility — reassure and praise their prenatal care.

8.3 Prevention and Adjunctive Therapy

  • Prevent Rh sensitization with Rh immune globulin (RhIg) given to the Rh-negative mother (prenatal/postpartum).
  • IVIG and tin-mesoporphyrin (heme oxygenase inhibitor) are adjuncts used with phototherapy per protocol.

9. Nursing Care Plan (Infant Under Phototherapy)

Assessment: 3-day-old term infant, TcB in the high-risk zone on the nomogram; phototherapy initiated; loose green stools; temperature 37.6 °C.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for injury (bilirubin encephalopathy) related to elevated unconjugated bilirubin; Risk for impaired skin integrity related to frequent stools and moisture.

Goals: TSB stays below the exchange threshold; normothermia maintained; skin intact; parents informed and involved.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Monitor TSB every 6–12 hours during phototherapy — visual estimation is invalid.
  2. Expose maximum skin; reposition frequently; maintain lamp distance per guidelines — maximizes light effect while preventing burns.
  3. Apply and check eye shields every 4–6 hours; remove during feedings — prevents corneal injury; provides stimulation.
  4. Feed frequently and increase fluids; assess for dehydration and hypocalcemia — compensates for losses.
  5. Meticulous perianal care and dry skin; no oily lotions — prevents excoriation/breakdown.
  6. Observe for kernicterus signs (hypotonia, high-pitched cry, temperature instability, lethargy) and report immediately — early detection prevents permanent damage.
  7. Evaluate: bilirubin decreasing toward the low-risk zone; temperature stable; skin intact; parents verbalize understanding.

11. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • On admission of any jaundiced newborn: obtain TSB or TcB, plot on the hour-specific nomogram, weigh, assess feeding and stooling, and check the prenatal history (maternal blood group, Coombs).
  • For an infant under phototherapy, follow the checklist; draw ordered bilirubin levels and notify the physician of values crossing nomogram thresholds.
  • Assist with exchange transfusion: keep NPO, maintain IV access, prepare the (double-volume) blood product cross-matched to the mother, document every mL exchanged, vital signs, and observed reactions; provide family support.
  • On discharge: schedule bilirubin follow-up (rebound), teach the family feeding frequency and warning signs (lethargy, high-pitched cry, poor feeding).

12. Case Study (Jaundice at 48 Hours)

Scenario: A 48-hour-old term infant, blood type A (mother O, Rh positive), develops jaundice to the chest. TcB 13.8 mg/dL; TSB 14.2 mg/dL; Coombs weakly positive; RBC spherocytosis noted.

  1. Classify the onset. (Jaundice on day 2 — the critical warning sign is onset within the first 24 hours; at 48 h this is still within the high-risk pathway for hemolytic disease — investigate hemolysis.)
  2. Which type of jaundice is most likely? (ABO hemolytic disease — maternal O with infant A, spherocytosis, weakly positive Coombs.)
  3. Planned treatment? (Early phototherapy; monitor TSB q6–12 h; IVIG per protocol; exchange transfusion only if phototherapy fails.)
  4. Which nursing observation suggests early bilirubin encephalopathy? (Lethargy, hypotonia, high-pitched cry, temperature instability.)

14. Key Points

  • Jaundice = accumulated bilirubin; unconjugated type dominates in newborns; only ~half of infants with elevated levels are visibly jaundiced.
  • Physiologic: onset after 24 h; formula-fed term peak ~5–6 mg/dL (day 2–5); breastfed 7–14 mg/dL; preterm 10–12 mg/dL.
  • Pathologic if: onset <24 h, bilirubin rising ≥5 mg/dL/day, positive Coombs, maternal group O + non-O infant, sibling history.
  • Phototherapy: max skin exposure, reposition, eye shields (close lids first, check q4–6 h, remove at feeding), temperature + lamp distance, increased fluids, perianal care, chart details; watch rebound after stopping.
  • Exchange transfusion: double-volume (~85 mL/kg), umbilical vein catheter, NPO, monitor volumes/vitals/reactions, document cumulative.
  • Early kernicterus signs: lethargy, hypotonia, high-pitched cry, temperature instability → report immediately.
