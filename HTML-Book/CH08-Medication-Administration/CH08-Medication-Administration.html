Chapter 8 — Medication Administration and Pediatric Dose Calculations

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Part I — Foundations of Pediatric Practical Care, Chapter 8

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain why pediatric dosing is weight-based and why it differs from adult dosing.
  2. Calculate a safe mg/kg dose, verify it against the recommended range, and convert it to mL of the available preparation.
  3. State when body surface area (BSA) dosing is used and calculate BSA with the Mosteller formula.
  4. Apply the safety "rights," two-identifier verification, and high-alert drug double-checking.
  5. Administer oral and intramuscular medication using atraumatic, aspiration-safe techniques.

2. Placement Box

4. Why Pediatric Dosing Is Different

  • Neonates and preterm infants are especially vulnerable: immature liver enzyme systems, lower plasma protein concentrations, and immature kidney function.
  • Beyond the newborn period, children metabolize many drugs more rapidly than adults, so they may need larger doses per kilogram and/or more frequent administration — important in pain control.
  • Doses are therefore expressed in mg/kg per dose or per day, and some drugs (e.g., chemotherapy) are dosed by BSA in mg/m².
  • BSA vs weight: an infant who is shorter and weighs less has relatively more BSA than expected from the weight.

5. Weight-Based Dose Calculation

General formula:

  • Dose per day = weight (kg) × recommended mg/kg/day
  • Dose per single administration = weight (kg) × recommended mg/kg/dose (or divide the daily dose by the number of doses/day)
  • Volume to give (mL) = ordered dose (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL)

5.1 Worked Examples

Example 1 (classic — bank Q14 style): Child weighs 22 lb; recommend 20–40 mg/kg/day divided every 8 h.

  • Convert: 22 lb ÷ 2.2 = 10 kg.
  • Daily range: 10 × 20 = 200 mg to 10 × 40 = 400 mg/day ✓ (safe range 200–400 mg/day).
  • Per dose (3 doses/day): 67–133 mg per dose.

Example 2 (paracetamol): 8-kg infant; 10–15 mg/kg/dose q4–6h, max 5 doses/day.

  • 8 × 12.5 (midrange) = 100 mg/dose.
  • Available syrup 160 mg/5 mL (32 mg/mL) → 100 ÷ 32 ≈ 3.1 mL per dose (give with an oral syringe in mL only).

Example 3 (antibiotic per day vs per dose): Amoxicillin 40 mg/kg/day divided q8h for a 12-kg child.

  • 12 × 40 = 480 mg/day → 160 mg/dose q8h.
  • Suspension 250 mg/5 mL (50 mg/mL) → 160 ÷ 50 = 3.2 mL q8h.

Teaching: always verify the calculated dose falls within the safe range before giving; if it does not, or if the preparation/route is doubtful, check with the prescriber before proceeding — the nurse is legally liable for the drug they administer.

6. Body Surface Area (BSA) Dosing

  • Used for drugs where metabolic rate matters (e.g., chemotherapy); expressed as mg/m².
  • Mosteller formula: BSA (m²) = √[(height cm × weight kg) ÷ 3600].
  • The West nomogram is a graphic alternative.
  • Worked example: height 90 cm, weight 15 kg → √[(90 × 15) ÷ 3600] = √(0.375) = 0.61 m². A drug ordered at 50 mg/m² = 50 × 0.61 = ≈ 30.6 mg.

7. Medication Safety Before Giving

  • Identify with two identifiers (e.g., name and medical record number or birth date); the parent can verify identity for an infant or nonverbal child.
  • Apply the rights of medication administration (right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, reason) and independently recheck dose calculations.
  • High-alert medications (e.g., antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants, chemotherapy, insulin, epinephrine, opioids, sedation agents) must be double-checked by a second nurse before administration.
  • A misplaced decimal point can cause a 10-fold or greater dosing error.
  • "Look-alike, sound-alike" drug names (e.g., DOBUTamine/DOPamine, predniSONE/prednisoLONE) use Tall Man lettering to emphasize differences.
  • If part of an oral dose is lost by spitting/vomiting, do not automatically repeat — consult the provider; repeat dosing may cause overdose.

8. Oral Administration

  • The oral route is preferred for ease of administration.
  • Measurement: the most accurate device is the plastic disposable oral syringe; the AAP recommends dosing liquid medications in milliliters only, never in teaspoons. Cups (esp. <5 mL) and household spoons are inaccurate; droppers vary with viscosity.
  • Infant technique: hold the infant semireclining; place the tip of the syringe in the pocket between the lower teeth and cheek; give small increments, allowing swallowing between each; never aim straight at the back of the throat (aspiration risk).
  • NURSING TIP: blowing a small puff of air in the face of an infant up to 11 months old frequently elicits a swallow reflex.
  • Do not add medicine to the infant's formula bottle (child may refuse the formula, and the full dose may not be taken).
  • Crushing: only crush when a liquid is unavailable; do not crush enteric-coated or sustained/slow-release products (overdose risk); split only scored tablets; a half dose can be made by dissolving a tablet in 5 mL of water and giving 2.5 mL.
  • Atraumatic tips: mix with a small amount (≈5 mL) of sweet substance; avoid essential foods; give a "chaser"; pinch the nose + straw works by blocking smell; give choices to the child to increase cooperation.

9. Intramuscular (IM) Administration

Volumes (single site): maximum ~1 mL in small children/older infants; small infants ≤ 0.5 mL; larger children approach adult volumes as muscle mass grows.

Recommended site (CDC/WHO/AAP): vastus lateralis for infants:

  • Landmarks: greater trochanter and knee; divide the distance into thirds and inject into the middle third; needle inserted perpendicular to the knee (22–25 gauge, ⅝–1 inch).
  • Ventrogluteal is suitable for children and infants from 2 months.

Technique details:

  • Use a tuberculin syringe (0.01-mL graduations) for volumes <1 mL and low-dose syringes for <0.1 mL.
  • Do not use an air bubble flush when giving <1 mL — it adds extra drug; dead space in the hub is not part of the calibrated dose.
  • Use the Z-track method for irritating drugs (e.g., iron dextran, tetanus toxoid) to prevent tracking into subcutaneous tissue.
  • Needle gauge: smallest that safely delivers (25–30 gauge = least discomfort); larger gauges for viscous drugs.

Ear drops (child ≥ 3 years): pull the pinna upward and backward (bank Q15).

10. Nursing Care Plan (Medication Safety)

Assessment: an 18-month-old about to receive the first dose of an order written "80 mg PO q8h"; weight 8 kg; drug range 20–40 mg/kg/day.

Risk analysis: dose/day = 80 × 3 = 240 mg/day; 8 kg × 40 mg/kg = 320 mg/day max — dose is within range (240 < 320). Safe to give at ~30 mg/kg/day.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for injury related to possible medication error.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Verify two identifiers and the order with the parent present — prevents wrong-patient error.
  2. Recalculate using the weight and published range — the nurse legally verifies every dose.
  3. Double-check if the drug is high-alert — reduces catastrophic error.
  4. Measure with an oral syringe in mL — accurate small-dose delivery.
  5. Give semireclining, small increments, suck-swallow pacing — prevents aspiration.
  6. Evaluate: documented dose, time, and response; no error occurred.

12. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Give IM immunizations (CH09) at the vastus lateralis in infants; change sites for repeated doses; record site, lot, and route.
  • For analgesic orders after surgery, use weight-based dose, reassess pain ≥30–60 min after administration.
  • Document every dose in the medication administration record; verify the next dose time to avoid double dosing.

13. Case Study (Dose Verification)

Ordered: paracetamol 90 mg PO q6h for an 11-kg child with fever (band: 10–15 mg/kg/dose, max 5 doses/day).

  1. Safe range per dose: 11 × 10 = 110 mg to 11 × 15 = 165 mg. The ordered 90 mg is BELOW the range — is it dangerous? No, but it is sub-therapeutic; the nurse should verify with the prescriber whether the dose was intended (perhaps written for a different weight).
  2. What if the dose had been 160 mg/dose q6h? Day total = 640 mg; 75 mg/kg/day = 825 mg max — acceptable, but as a single 14.5 mg/kg dose within band.
  3. Available syrup 120 mg/5 mL, ordered 90 mg → 90 ÷ 24 = 3.75 mL.

Teaching point: always compute the per-kg dose and compare with the published range before giving; document the calculation.

15. Key Points

  • Pediatric doses are weight-based (mg/kg); some drugs use BSA (mg/m²).
  • Convert lb → kg (÷2.2), compute mg/kg/day and mg/kg/dose, verify against the safe range, then convert to mL using the concentration.
  • Use two identifiers; double-check high-alert drugs with a second nurse; a decimal error = 10× dosed.
  • Oral liquids: dose in mL only with an oral syringe; semireclining position, side-of-cheek pocket.
  • IM: vastus lateralis in infants (middle third), ≤1 mL per site (0.5 mL in small infants), no air flush <1 mL, Z-track for irritants.
