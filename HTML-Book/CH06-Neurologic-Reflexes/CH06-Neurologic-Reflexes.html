1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Perform the basic neurologic screen: level of consciousness, pupil responses, posture, motor tone, and fontanelle assessment.
  2. Elicit the newborn reflexes correctly (sucking, rooting, grasp, Babinski, Moro, tonic neck, dance/step) using the described stimulation.
  3. State the normal time of appearance and disappearance of each primitive reflex.
  4. Recognize abnormal findings: asymmetric or absent Moro, persistent primitive reflexes (obligatory tonic neck, hyperactive Moro), and a positive Babinski persisting after 1 year.
  5. Complete the lab and hospital steps of the neurologic/reflex assessment using the checklist.

2. Placement Box

4. The Basic Neurologic Screen

A useful screen before reflex testing:

  • Level of consciousness (LOC): alert, lethargic, obtunded, comatose; observation periods according to age.
  • Pupils: equal, round, reactive to light; note asymmetric or one dilated unresponsive pupil (emergency).
  • Posture and movement: spontaneous activity (none in deep sleep), symmetric movement of all limbs; flaccidity or spasticity.
  • Fontanelles: anterior fontanelle flat, soft, open (closes ~12–18 months); a sunken fontanelle suggests dehydration [Ch 17]; a tense/bulging fontanelle suggests increased intracranial pressure.
  • Cranial nerves: tested at age-appropriate level (e.g., visual red reflex, pupillary response, sucking, facial symmetry).

Safety / Red flag: asymmetric pupils in a child with altered consciousness, a tense bulging fontanelle, or seizures are emergencies — report immediately.

6. Eliciting the Key Reflexes (Technique Notes)

  • Sucking and rooting: place a nipple or nonlatex gloved finger in the mouth (vigorous suck expected — weak suck should be reported); root by stroking the cheek and noting the head turning to the stimulated side and sucking.
  • Moro: sudden jarring or change in equilibrium (e.g., mild head drop with support); the strongest response is seen in the first 2 months; an asymmetric or partial Moro reflex is abnormal (see Section 7).
  • Tonic neck: after 2 months becomes obligatory for the "fencing" posture; an obligatory tonic neck reflex at any age is abnormal.
  • Grasp: firm but gentle pressure on the palm/soles produces flexion.
  • Babinski: stroke the outer sole upward from the heel and across the ball of the foot; toes hyperextend and the hallux dorsiflexes then fans in infants — normal; a positive Babinski is significant when it persists after 1 year, when it reflects pyramidal tract dysfunction.
  • Dance/step: hold the infant so the sole of the foot touches a hard surface — reciprocal flexion/extension simulates walking.

7. Clinical Significance and Abnormal Findings

  • Asymmetric or partial Moro suggests brachial plexus injury, clavicle fracture, or CNS dysfunction; it should be reported.
  • Persistent primitive reflexes are the earliest clue to cerebral palsy: obligatory tonic neck reflex at any age, and non-obligatory or hyperactivity of the Moro, plantar, and palmar grasp reflexes beyond the normal age.
  • Asymmetric tonic neck reflex (evoked by flexing or extending the head) persisting abnormally has diagnostic significance.
  • Absence of tonic neck reflex and asymmetric posturing (except the tonic neck reflex) are reportable findings in the newborn.
  • Exaggerated/hyperactive Moro (e.g., in drug-exposed newborns) and inability to coordinate swallowing should be noted.
  • In a child, a fluctuating Babinski reflex is often observed after seizures.

8. Nursing Care Plan (Neurologic Assessment Focus)

Assessment: a 1-day-old newborn with a weak suck, no rooting, and asymmetric Moro response on the right arm.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for injury / Neurologic status: risk for altered intracranial (assessment monitoring) related to suspected neurologic dysfunction.

Goals: Continue monitoring and report changes promptly; the parents will understand the referral.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Elicit reflexes consistently in a quiet state and by the same method — reproducible baseline.
  2. Document precisely: "Moro: asymmetric — right arm absent" — prompts investigation (nerve vs CNS).
  3. Re-check before any major procedure and notify the physician of asymmetry/persistence — early diagnosis.
  4. Support parental understanding without alarming — explain that a weak suck may be transient but warrants watching.
  5. Evaluate: interval neurologic re-assessment completed; findings documented and reported.

10. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Repeat the checklist on the real newborn in a quiet state, with warm hands, before feeding if possible.
  • Elicit the Moro reflex at the end to avoid the startle disturbing the rest of the examination.
  • Document using the ward's admission sheet: "Describe reflexes observed: Moro, sucking, Babinski, plantar, and other age-appropriate reflex responses".
  • Report asymmetric Moro, weak/absent suck, or persistent primitive reflexes immediately.

11. Case Study (Reflex Assessment)

A 2-day-old full-term newborn, examined before discharge. Sucking and rooting present and strong; palmar and plantar grasp present; Babinski positive. At the end of the examination the nurse raises the head slightly and allows it to drop — the newborn extends and abducts both arms, fingers fan, with the index and thumb forming a C, then adducts and flexes; both sides respond equally and the baby cries briefly.

  • Why was the Moro reflex left for the end? (Startle may make the infant cry and disturb the rest of the exam.)
  • Is the response normal? (Yes — symmetric Moro with extinction after flexion; expected up to 3–4 months, strongest in the first 2 months.)
  • Which finding would have been abnormal? (Asymmetric response — e.g., absent arm movement — suggesting brachial plexus/CNS injury.)
  • The positive Babinski is expected at this age; when would it be significant? (After 1 year of age.)

13. Key Points

  • Use the newborn reflex table for technique, expected response, and disappearance age.
  • Elicit the Moro reflex last in the examination.
  • An asymmetric/partial Moro and weak/absent suck should be reported.
  • Persistent primitive reflexes (obligatory tonic neck, hyperactive Moro beyond normal age) are early clues to cerebral palsy.
  • Babinski is normal in infancy; a positive Babinski after 1 year is a pyramidal sign.
