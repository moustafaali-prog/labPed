Part I — Foundations of Pediatric Practical Care, Chapter 7

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain why fluid requirements per kilogram are higher in infants than in older children.
  2. Calculate daily maintenance fluid and calorie requirements using the Holliday–Segar method.
  3. Classify dehydration (mild, moderate, severe) and prescribe the correct first-line therapy (oral rehydration vs IV).
  4. Calculate ORS volumes for replacement therapy and IV infusion flow rates (drops/min).
  5. Monitor IV therapy, intake/output, urine output, and daily weight in the hospitalized child.

2. Placement Box

4. Why Children Differ in Fluid Balance

  • Infants have a proportionately greater amount of body water than adults, so their fluid intake requirement per kilogram is greater, and they are at higher risk of fluid loss.
  • Kidney immaturity limits water and electrolyte conservation; insensible losses are relatively larger in infants (larger body surface area per kg).
  • Calorie needs for growth are high, so very young children need 100 kcal/kg/day for the first 10 kg — matching the 100 mL/kg/day figure of the Holliday–Segar method.

5. Maintenance Fluid and Calorie Requirements (Holliday–Segar Method)

Parenteral maintenance fluid for a hospitalized child is planned by this method:

Body weightFluid per kg per dayExample
First 10 kg100 mL/kg/day10 kg × 100 = 1000 mL/day
Second 10 kg50 mL/kg/day10 kg × 50 = 500 mL/day
Each kg above 20 kg20 mL/kg/day(weight − 20) × 20 mL/day

Total for the day = sum of the three columns. The same 100/50/20 pattern is used for maintenance calories (kcal/day).

Worked example: A 25-kg child needs:

  • First 10 kg → 1000 mL/day
  • Second 10 kg → 500 mL/day
  • Remaining 5 kg → 5 × 20 = 100 mL/day
  • Total = 1600 mL/day ✓

Daily fluid requirements by age (practical guide): newborn/infant about 140–160 mL/kg/day; child 100 mL/kg/day for the first 10 kg then as above.

5.1 Worked Calculations (Holiday–Segar)

  1. 8-kg infant: 8 × 100 = 800 mL/day (≈ 33 mL/hr).
  2. 12-kg toddler: (10 × 100) + (2 × 50) = 1000 + 100 = 1100 mL/day (≈ 46 mL/hr).
  3. 40-kg school child: 1000 + 500 + (20 × 20) = 1000 + 500 + 400 = 1900 mL/day (≈ 79 mL/hr) — plus calories similarly.

6. Dehydration: Classification and First-line Therapy

Use the AAP dehydration classification and therapy plan:

DegreeWeight loss / signsRehydration therapyReplacement of stool lossesMaintenance
Mild (5–6%)Increased thirst; slightly dry buccal mucous membranesORS 50 mL/kg within 4 hoursORS 10 mL/kg (infants) or 150–250 mL per diarrheal stool (older children)Continue breastfeeding/formula; usual diet for solids
Moderate (7–9%)Loss of skin turgor; dry buccal mucous membranes; sunken eyes; sunken fontanelORS 100 mL/kg within 4 hoursSame as aboveSame as above
Severe (>9%)Moderate signs plus rapid thready pulse, cyanosis, rapid breathing, lethargy, comaIV fluids (lactated Ringer), 40 mL/kg until pulse and level of consciousness normalize; then ORS 50–100 mL/kgSame as aboveSame as above
  • ORT is the treatment of choice for most cases of dehydration; it is more effective, safer, less painful, and less costly than IV therapy.
  • Vomiting is not a contraindication to ORS — give small frequent amounts (spoon or small syringe, 5–10 mL every 1–2 minutes).
  • ORS should contain about 75 mmol/L sodium and 13.5 g/L glucose (standard solutions: Pedialyte, Infalyte, Ricelyte). Do not use juice, carbonated drinks, gelatin — they are high in carbohydrate and low in electrolytes.
  • Homemade ORS: 1 L water + 8 tsp sugar + 1 tsp salt (use only if no commercial ORS).
  • For severe dehydration and shock, IV fluids (normal saline or lactated Ringer) are initiated; the classic initial bolus is 20 mL/kg, reassessing after each bolus.
  • NURSING ALERT: clear fluids (juices, soft drinks, gelatin) do not help diarrhea; a BRAT diet is contraindicated.

7. Intravenous Fluid Therapy in the Hospital

Patients usually requiring IV fluids: severe dehydration, uncontrollable vomiting, inability to drink (extreme fatigue, coma), and severe gastric distention.

  • The IV solution is usually a saline solution with 5% dextrose (D5); sodium bicarbonate may be added because acidosis accompanies severe dehydration.
  • Rapid replacement is contraindicated in hypertonic dehydration because of the risk of water intoxication.
  • Frequency of assessment: monitor the correct concentration, flow rate, infusion site, and intake/output; weigh the child daily — accurate weight is one of the best indicators of fluid status.

7.1 Drop-Rate Calculations (Worked Examples)

Formula: Drops/min = (Total mL × Drop factor) ÷ Minutes

  • Macro drip sets deliver 15–20 gtts/mL; microdrip delivers 60 gtts/mL (so, drop rate in gtts/min = mL/hr).
  • Example (macro 20): 500 mL over 8 hours = 500 × 20 ÷ 480 = ≈ 21 drops/min.
  • Example (microdrip 60): 800 mL over 24 hours = 33 mL/hr → 33 drops/min.
  • Teach: 1000 mL over 24 h with a microdrip = 1000/24 ≈ 42 mL/hr ≈ 42 gtts/min.

Recheck the electric pump by drop counting for 1 minute and verify against the written order; investigate any discrepancy.

8. Monitoring Fluid Status: Urine Output and I/O

  • Minimal acceptable urine output: 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr in children; oliguria is urine output < 1 mL/kg/hr and anuria = no urine.
  • Maintain strict intake/output record and weigh daily; monitor capillary refill, sensorium, skin turgor, oral mucosa, and presence of tears every 4–8 hours.
  • The most reliable indicators of volume status are capillary refill, alterations in sensorium, and urinary output.

9. Electrolytes and Calories at the Bedside

  • Sodium (Na): main cation of the extracellular fluid, 135–145 mmol/L. Low-sodium ORS/fluids are used in maintenance to avoid hypernatremia.
  • Potassium (K): 3.5–5.5 mmol/L; only add K to IV fluids when urine output is adequate; never give K by IV push.
  • Calories: 1 g carbohydrate ≈ 4 kcal; glucose is 3.4 kcal/g. Use the 100/50/20 method for maintenance calories of parenteral support.

10. Nursing Care Plan (Dehydration — Risk for Deficient Fluid Volume)

Assessment: an 8-month-old with 2 days of watery diarrhea, 6 stools in 8 hours, decreased urine output (2 wet diapers/day), sunken eyes, dry mucous membranes, slightly decreased skin turgor, weight unchanged from 2 weeks prior.

NANDA diagnosis: Deficient fluid volume related to excessive fluid losses (diarrhea) as evidenced by decreased urine output, dry mucous membranes, and sunken eyes.

Assessment classification: mild dehydration (weight stable, tears present).

Goals: Rehydrate with ORS; restore urine output to ≥ 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr; maintain daily weight.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Give ORS 50 mL/kg within 4 hours in 5–10 mL amounts every 2–5 min — replaces deficit slowly and reduces vomiting.
  2. Continue breastfeeding during and after rehydration — supports energy and fluid.
  3. Replace stool losses: 10 mL/kg ORS per diarrheal stool — prevents recurrence of dehydration.
  4. Record I/O, count wet diapers, weigh daily — objective evaluation of rehydration.
  5. Avoid juices, gelatin, and caffeinated drinks — they worsen the diarrhea.
  6. Evaluate: hydration restored (tears, moist mucosa, urine output ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr), plan for early refeeding.

12. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Obtain an accurate admission weight (undressed, same scale) before ordering fluids.
  • For severe dehydration, assist with the 20 mL/kg LR/NS bolus per protocol and reassess after each bolus.
  • Teach the mother ORS technique (spoon/syringe, 5–10 mL, every 1–2 min), stool-loss replacement, and to bring the child back if urine output falls or lethargy develops.
  • Document intake (including ORS) and output (diarrhea/vomiting/urine) carefully; report urine output < 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr.

13. Case Studies

A) Maintenance fluid (Holliday–Segar): A 12-kg toddler is NPO for surgery. Ordered IV: D5 ½ NS plus 20 mEq/L KCl. Calculate the 24-h rate. Answer: (10×100)+(2×50) = 1100 mL/day = 46 mL/hr.

B) ORS for moderate dehydration: A 10-kg child with sunken eyes, dry mucosa, loss of skin turgor (moderate). Answer: ORS 100 mL/kg ÷ 4 h = 1000 mL over 4 hours; then maintenance therapy and stool-loss replacement.

C) IV drip rate: Order 500 mL D5 ½ NS over 8 h with a microdrip. Answer: 62.5 mL/hr → ≈ 63 gtts/min (microdrip = mL/hr).

15. Key Points

  • Use the Holliday–Segar 100/50/20 method for maintenance fluid and calories.
  • Mild (5–6%) → ORS 50 mL/kg/4 h; moderate (7–9%) → ORS 100 mL/kg/4 h; severe (>9%) → IV LR 40 mL/kg until pulse/LOC normalize, then ORS.
  • ORS: 75 mmol/L Na, 13.5 g/L glucose; give small frequent amounts even if vomiting; replace each stool loss.
  • IV bolus 20 mL/kg NS or LR for shock, reassessing after each bolus.
  • Never give K by IV push; only add K when urine output is adequate.
  • Monitor urine output ≥ 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr and weigh daily.
