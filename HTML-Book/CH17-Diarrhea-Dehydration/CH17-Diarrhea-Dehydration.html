Chapter 17 — Acute Diarrhea, Vomiting, and Dehydration

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Book: Clinical Pediatric Nursing: A Comprehensive Practical Guide

Part IV — Medical Ward, Chapter 17

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. State the common causes of acute gastroenteritis (rotavirus is the leading cause of serious gastroenteritis) and its pathophysiology (secretory vs invasive diarrhea → dehydration, acidosis, shock).
  2. Classify dehydration as mild (5–6%), moderate (7–9%), or severe (>9%) and identify the signs of each.
  3. Apply the rehydration protocol: ORS 50 mL/kg (mild) and 100 mL/kg (moderate) within 4 hours; IV lactated Ringer 40 mL/kg for severe dehydration until pulse/consciousness improve.
  4. Provide nursing care: small frequent ORS feedings even while vomiting, ongoing stool-loss replacement, I&O and daily weight monitoring, early reintroduction of diet, breastfeeding continuation, and prevention education (hand hygiene, rotavirus vaccine).

4. Definition, Causes, and Transmission

  • Most pathogens causing diarrhea spread by the fecal-oral route (contaminated food/water) or person-to-person close contact; risk factors: lack of clean water, crowding, poor hygiene, nutritional deficiency, poor sanitation. Infants are especially susceptible because their immune systems have not acquired protective antibodies.
  • Worldwide, acute gastroenteritis is caused by viruses, bacteria, and parasites; rotavirus is the most important cause of serious gastroenteritis in children, spreads fecal-orally, and almost all children are infected at least once by age 5; it is the most common cause of diarrhea-associated hospitalization.
  • Common bacterial pathogens: Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, Shigella (raw/undercooked food or contaminated water).
  • Antibiotic-associated diarrhea: antibiotics alter intestinal flora; *C. difficile* is the most common bacterial overgrowth, usually acquired in health care facilities (20% community/daycare).

5. Pathophysiology

  • Pathogens attach to mucosal cells; diarrhea is secretory (noninvasive, toxin-producing, noninflammatory — the most common) when an enterotoxin acts after mucosal attachment, or systemic (invasive) when the organism penetrates the mucosa.
  • The most serious immediate disturbances of severe diarrheal disease: dehydration, acid-base imbalance with acidosis, and shock when dehydration seriously impairs circulation.

6. Clinical Manifestations — Assessment of Dehydration

DegreeSigns and Symptoms
Mild (5–6%)Increased thirst; slightly dry buccal mucous membranes
Moderate (7–9%)Loss of skin turgor; dry buccal mucous membranes; sunken eyes; sunken fontanel
Severe (>9%)Signs of moderate dehydration plus one of: rapid thready pulse, cyanosis, rapid breathing, lethargy, coma

If no signs of dehydration are present, rehydration therapy is not necessary — proceed with maintenance therapy and replacement of stool losses.

Additional moderate–severe findings: decreased tear production, sunken orbits, dry mucous membranes, listlessness/lethargy, skin tenting; heart rate increases in moderate dehydration but blood pressure stays normal until it decreases in severe dehydration.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Usually clinical (history of diarrhea/vomiting + examination); inspect stool color/consistency, auscultate hyperactive bowel sounds in gastroenteritis vs hypoactive in obstruction, palpate for tenderness.
  • Identify pathogens when indicated (stool culture/PCR) especially for persistent, bloody, or daycare-associated diarrhea.

8. Therapeutic Management

8.1 Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) — the mainstay

  • ORS should contain 75 mmol/L sodium chloride and 13.5 g/L glucose (standard products: Pedialyte, Infalyte); tap water, milk, undiluted fruit juice, soup, and broth are NOT appropriate for rehydration.
  • Mild dehydration: ORS 50 mL/kg within 4 hours.
  • Moderate dehydration: ORS 100 mL/kg within 4 hours; after reevaluation continue ORS if still dehydrated.
  • Vomiting is NOT a contraindication to ORS: give ORS at frequent intervals in small amounts — 5–10 mL increments (spoon or small syringe) every 2–5 minutes for young children; ORS may also be given by NG or gastrostomy tube.
  • Replacement of ongoing stool losses: ORS 10 mL/kg (infants) or 150–250 mL (older children) for each diarrheal stool; if stool volume is unknown, about 10 mL/kg is the guide.
  • Maintenance nutrition: breastfeeding continues; regular formula if tolerated (lactose-free formula for suspected lactose intolerance); children on solids continue their usual diet; early reintroduction of the normal diet lessens duration of illness and improves weight gain.
  • Dietary cautions: avoid high-carbohydrate fluids (fruit juice, Kool-Aid — low in electrolytes, decrease stool transit time); discourage milk/high-sugar fluids during the acute phase.

8.2 IV (Parenteral) Therapy

  • IV fluids are for severe dehydration, uncontrollable vomiting, inability to drink, or failure of ORT.
  • Severe dehydration: IV lactated Ringer (or normal saline) 40 mL/kg until pulse and state of consciousness return to normal, then ORS 50–100 mL/kg can be given. Initial bolus 20 mL/kg of NS or LR, then reassess hydration.
  • Parenteral rehydration proceeds in phases: initial deficit replacement, maintenance, and replacement of ongoing losses.

9. Vomiting Management

  • Vomiting is a symptom of some underlying condition; therapeutic management usually involves slow oral rehydration and possibly antiemetics.
  • Withhold oral feeding for 1–2 hours after emesis, then start ORT; give the infant/child 0.5–2 oz (about 15–60 mL) of ORS every 15 minutes depending on age and size; most retain small amounts; larger amounts as improvement occurs.
  • If oral rehydration is impossible because of continued nausea/vomiting → IV fluids; antiemetics (e.g., ondansetron — preferred over promethazine for fewer side effects) may control vomiting.
  • Bilious vomiting is the main symptom of bowel obstruction (e.g., malrotation with volvulus) — emergency.

10. Worked Scenario Examples (Calculations)

Example 1 — Moderate dehydration, ORS volume : A 10-kg toddler has moderate dehydration (7–9%).

  • ORS needed in 4 h = 100 mL/kg × 10 kg = 1000 mL over 4 hours.
  • Ongoing stool-replacement = 10 mL/kg per stool = 100 mL ORS per diarrheal stool.

Example 2 — Mild dehydration, ORS volume: A 6-kg infant with mild dehydration.

  • ORS in 4 h = 50 mL/kg × 6 kg = 300 mL over 4 hours.
  • Give 5–10 mL every 2–5 min by syringe to minimize vomiting.

Example 3 — Severe dehydration, IV bolus: A 12-kg child with severe dehydration (rapid thready pulse, lethargy).

  • IV lactated Ringer 40 mL/kg = 12 × 40 = 480 mL until pulse and consciousness normalize, then ORS 50–100 mL/kg.

Example 4 — Maintenance (review from CH7): Holliday–Segar: for 10 kg = 1000 mL/day + ongoing stool losses each replaced 1:1 with ORS (10 mL/kg per stool).

11. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 8-month-old, 3 days of watery stools (10/day), vomiting after feeds, decreased tears, dry mucous membranes, sunken fontanel, skin turgor tents; temperature 38.5 °C; input very low.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Deficient fluid volume related to diarrhea and vomiting.
  • Imbalanced nutrition: less than body requirements related to poor intake and losses.
  • Risk for impaired skin integrity (diaper area).

Goals: Rehydrated within 4 h; urine output adequate; no worsening; skin intact; parent competent in ORT.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Give ORS 100 mL/kg over 4 h in 5–10 mL increments by spoon/syringe every 2–5 min; continue despite vomiting — effective rehydration even with emesis.
  2. Replace each diarrheal stool with ORS 10 mL/kg — prevents recurrent deficit.
  3. Assess hydration and daily weight; strict I & O; assess skin turgor, oral mucosa, tears, fontanel every 4–8 h; daily weight is one of the best indicators of fluid status — early detection of failure.
  4. Monitor urine output (0.5–1 mL/kg/h adequate) — end-organ perfusion gauge.
  5. Continue breastfeeding; resume formula/usual diet early after rehydration — nutrition, lessens illness duration.
  6. Skin care: barrier ointment in the diaper area; frequent diaper changes — prevents excoriation.
  7. IV fluids if severe dehydration, uncontrollable vomiting, or failed ORT; initial bolus 20 mL/kg NS/LR then reassess — restores circulation.
  8. Prevention education: hand hygiene; rotavirus vaccination (first dose by 15 weeks of age); avoid sharing cups/utensils — breaks the fecal-oral cycle.
  9. Evaluate: 4-h weight gain, urine output adequate, tolerates feeding, parent demonstrates ORS technique.

13. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • On admission, grade dehydration, weigh the child, initiate ORT (or IV LR 40 mL/kg if severe), and start strict I&O with stool-loss replacement.
  • Monitor weight trends and reassess hydration every 4–8 h; escalate to IV when ORT fails.
  • Teach the mother ORS preparation/dosing, early feeding, signs to return, and prevention (hand hygiene + rotavirus vaccine).

14. Case Study (Severe Dehydration)

Scenario: A 2-year-old (12 kg) has 2 days of profuse watery diarrhea and frequent vomiting. He is listless, with a rapid thready pulse, deep rapid breathing, dry mouth, and tenting skin; no urine output in 8 hours.

  1. How do you grade the dehydration? (Severe (>9%): moderate signs + rapid thready pulse and rapid breathing/lethargy.)
  2. What is the first treatment? (IV lactated Ringer 40 mL/kg = 480 mL until pulse and consciousness improve; then ORS; initial bolus 20 mL/kg then reassess.)
  3. Why is acidosis present? (Severe diarrheal disease → dehydration plus acid-base imbalance with acidosis from bicarbonate loss; shock when circulation is impaired.)
  4. After stabilization, what fluids? (ORS 50–100 mL/kg, 5–10 mL increments; replace each stool loss 10 mL/kg; keep breastfeeding.)
  5. Which vaccine prevents the leading viral cause? (Rotavirus vaccine — rotavirus is the most important cause of serious gastroenteritis.)

16. Key Points

  • Acute gastroenteritis: viruses/bacteria/parasites, fecal-oral spread; rotavirus = leading viral cause; secretory diarrhea most common → dehydration, acidosis, shock.
  • Grading: mild 5–6% (thirst), moderate 7–9% (turgor loss, sunken eyes/fontanel), severe >9% (thready pulse, cyanosis, rapid breathing, lethargy/coma).
  • Rehydration: ORS 50 mL/kg (mild) or 100 mL/kg (moderate) over 4 h in 5–10 mL increments; replace stools 10 mL/kg; severe → IV LR 40 mL/kg then ORS; ORS composition Na 75 mmol/L, glucose 13.5 g/L.
  • Vomiting management: 1–2 h nothing by mouth then slow ORS; antiemetics (ondansetron preferred); IV if ORT fails.
  • Nursing: I&O, daily weight, urine output, skin care, early feeds/breastfeeding, prevention (hand hygiene, rotavirus vaccine).
  • Never give tap water, milk, juice, soup, or broth for rehydration.
