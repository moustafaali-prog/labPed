Chapter 10 — Core Ward Skills: Hand Hygiene, Atraumatic Care, Isolation, and Documentation

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Book: Clinical Pediatric Nursing: A Comprehensive Practical Guide

Part I — Foundations of Pediatric Practical Care, Chapter 10

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Perform correct hand hygiene (soap and water / alcohol-based) — the single most important infection-control practice.
  2. Apply Standard Precautions and identify when Airborne, Droplet, and Contact Precautions are added.
  3. Prepare a child and family for isolation and minimize the psychological stress of isolation.
  4. Apply the principles of atraumatic care (prevent/minimize physical and psychological distress; minimize parent–child separation; promote family-centered care).
  5. Document nursing assessment, interventions, weights, and intake/output correctly on the ward record and give a structured handover.

2. Placement Box

4. Hand Hygiene and Standard Precautions

  • Each day about 1 in 31 patients acquires at least one hospital-acquired infection (HAI); HAIs are preventable with meticulous hand washing, cleaning, and disposal.
  • Hand hygiene is the single most important practice to reduce transmission of infectious diseases in the health care setting; includes washing with soap and water and use of alcohol-based hand disinfectants.
  • Standard Precautions synthesize universal precautions (blood/body fluids) and body-substance isolation:
  • Use barrier PPE — gloves, goggles, gowns, and masks — for contact with blood, all body fluids/secretions/excretions (except sweat), nonintact skin, and mucous membranes.
  • Standard Precautions apply to all patients because unrecognized sources of infection exist.
  • Respiratory Hygiene/Cough Etiquette and safe injection practices are part of Standard Precautions: cover mouth/nose when coughing, mask coughing persons, 3 feet of space, tissues; use safety-engineered sharps.
  • Glove use: gloves do not provide complete protection — wash hands thoroughly after removing gloves; disposable-diaper plastic lining is a sufficient barrier, so gloves are adequate for routine diaper changes (add gown for explosive/liquid stools).
  • Sharps: dispose of all needles (uncapped, unbroken) in rigid, puncture-resistant containers near the point of use, placed out of children's reach; prefer needleless systems.
  • MRSA transmission is mainly patient-to-patient via the hands of health care providers; hand washing is the most critical infection-control practice.

6. Preparing the Child for Isolation (Pediatric Approach)

Admission to an isolation room increases all the stressors of hospitalization: further separation, loss of control, sensory deprivation, and strangers.

  • Preschoolers cannot understand cause-and-effect; they view isolation as punishment. Use a simple story: "You need to be in this special room to help you get better; the infection made you sick and you could not help that".
  • Prepare the child for what they will see, hear, and feel: show the mask, gloves, and gown and let the child "dress up" in them; playing with PPE lessens fear of staff entering.
  • Staff should introduce themselves and let the child see their faces before donning masks, so the child associates them with familiar people.
  • Provide appropriate play activities when the child's condition improves; keep explanations developmentally matched.

7. Principles of Atraumatic Care

Atraumatic care is therapeutic care that minimizes or eliminates the psychological and physical distress experienced by children and their families in the health care system (premise: "do no harm").

Box 30.1 — Principles :

  • Prevent or minimize physical stressors: pain, discomfort, immobility, sleep deprivation, inability to eat/drink, changes in elimination.
  • Avoid or reduce intrusive/painful procedures (multiple punctures, injections, urethral catheterization).
  • Reduce other physical distress: noise, smells, shivering, nausea, sleeplessness, restraints, skin trauma.
  • Control pain by frequent assessment plus pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions.
  • Prevent or minimize parent–child separation.
  • Promote family-centered care — treat the family as the patient; use core primary nursing.
  • Follow research on whether parents and children prefer to be together.
  • Promote a sense of control (tell the child what will happen, give choices, use distraction).

8. Documentation and Handover

  • Document objectively and immediately: admission assessment, weight and head circumference, vital signs, I/O (including ORS), medication doses (route, site, lot), isolation precautions, and "reflexes observed, fontanelle status" where relevant [Ch 2, 6, 7].
  • Weight daily — one of the best indicators of fluid status in children.
  • Use the structured SBAR handover: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation — especially during ward shift changes and when transferring an isolated child.
  • Legal note: the nurse is accountable for what they document and administer; record the exact time of medications and observations, and report any discrepancy with the written order.

9. Nursing Care Plan (Risk for Infection — Hand Environment)

Assessment: 2-year-old on the medical ward with rotavirus gastroenteritis and loose explosive stools; nursing staff rotate frequently.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for infection related to exposure to a multidrug-resistant organism / inadequate hand hygiene.

Goals: Prevent cross-infection on the ward; the child's course is uncomplicated; no staff/patient transmission.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Apply hand hygiene before and after every contact — single most important practice.
  2. Use Contact Precautions (gloves + gown) for diapered patient with rotavirus — interrupts fecal–oral route.
  3. Hand hygiene after glove removal because gloves fail to provide complete protection.
  4. Contain stool: gloves for routine changes, gown for explosive stools; dispose of diapers properly; clean environment.
  5. Prepare the preschooler for isolation with play ("dress up"), face-to-face introductions, and simple explanations — decreases fear misconceived as punishment.
  6. Evaluate: no secondary cases on the ward; family understands the isolation routine.

11. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Enter isolation rooms only with the correct PPE displayed on the door sign; dedicate equipment where required (stethoscope, thermometer).
  • At handover, clearly state the precautions for each child (e.g., "rotavirus — contact; measles — airborne, AIIR").
  • Reassure and re-explain isolation to the child each shift using play and age-appropriate language.

12. Case Study (Contact Precautions in an Isolation Room)

Scenario: A 3-year-old is admitted with measles-like symptoms and placed in the AIIR. A second child on the ward has rotavirus diarrhea.

  • Which precautions does the measles-suspected child need? (Airborne + standard; AIIR with special ventilation; N-95 respirator for staff.)
  • Which precautions does the rotavirus child need? (Contact + standard; gloves and gown.)
  • The preschooler with rotavirus keeps crying when nurses enter in gowns and masks. How would you prepare him? (Let him "dress up" in gloves/gown/mask; show faces before masking; simple explanation that the room helps him get better.)
  • After leaving the rotavirus room, what is the key step? (Remove PPE correctly and perform hand hygiene — gloves fail to provide complete protection.)

14. Key Points

  • Hand hygiene is the single most important infection-control practice.
  • Standard Precautions (PPE for blood, body fluids, nonintact skin, mucous membranes) apply to all patients.
  • Airborne (AIIR: measles, varicella, TB), Droplet (≤3 ft: Hib, meningococcus, pertussis, influenza), Contact (MRSA, C. difficile, rotavirus) — all added to Standard Precautions.
  • Isolated children — especially preschoolers — need preparation and play; they may view isolation as punishment.
  • Atraumatic care: minimize physical/psychological stressors, avoid painful procedures, minimize separation, promote family-centered care and sense of control.
  • Document objectively, weigh daily, and give structured SBAR handovers.
