Chapter 5 — Vital Signs in Infants and Children

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Part I — Foundations of Pediatric Practical Care, Chapter 5

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Take vital signs in the correct order for infants (respirations → pulse → temperature).
  2. Measure temperature by the appropriate route (axillary, tympanic, temporal artery, rectal indications) with the correct nursing alerts.
  3. Assess the apical pulse for children younger than 2–10 years and palpate the radial pulse in older children, counting a full minute.
  4. Count the respiratory rate by the correct observation (abdominal in infants; thoracic after 1 year) for a full minute.
  5. Measure blood pressure with the correct cuff (bladder width ≥ 40% of arm circumference) and interpret cuff-size errors.
  6. State the age-based normal value bands for HR, RR, temperature, and BP (newborn table from the sources).
  7. Perform the lab and hospital steps of vital-sign measurement using the checklist.

2. Placement Box

4. Temperature

Definition and norms: core temperature reflects blood flow to the hypothalamus. For rectal temperatures in children, 37.0–37.5°C (98.6–99.5°F) is an acceptable range where heat loss and production are balanced; for neonates a core temperature of 36.5–37.6°C (97.7–99.7°F) is desirable. In the neonate, temperature is monitored for thermoregulation adequacy, not just for fever.

Routes:

  • Axillary — recommended for infants younger than 1 month; nonintrusive, preferred when possible.
  • Tympanic membrane (ear) — takes precedence for children older than 2 years; meta-analysis shows wide variability — do not base a fever-without-focus diagnosis on tympanic readings alone.
  • Temporal artery — acceptable in all age-groups, correlates better with rectal than axillary/tympanic in studies.
  • Rectal — closest to core temperature but invasive; take the temperature of the young infant supine with legs flexed, or prone/side-lying; insert past the anal sphincter no more than 2.5 cm (1 inch); apply water-soluble jelly.

Nursing Alert: avoid the rectal route in the immunosuppressed child and in the child with diarrhea, a bleeding disorder, or a history of rectal surgery.

Nursing Alert: axillary temperature is lower than oral, but the difference varies too widely for a standard conversion — do not add a degree to the axillary reading. In neonates with fever, axillary alone should not be used for clinical decisions.

Order of measurement in infants (before disturbing): count respirations first, take the pulse next, then measure temperature; if the child is disturbed (crying), record the behavior along with the measurement.

5. Pulse (Heart Rate)

  • Assess the heart rate while the child is resting or sleeping; the rate in infants is much faster than in adults and varies with anxiety, fear, or crying.
  • In children younger than 2 years, always assess the apical pulse — the radial pulse is difficult to palpate accurately in this age group. For children younger than 10 years, auscultate the apical pulse with the stethoscope for a full minute; in older children palpate the radial pulse for a full minute.
  • Wait several seconds after placing the stethoscope on the chest for the rate to slow from fear, then count 1 full minute.
  • Count 1 full minute in infants/young children because of possible irregularities.
  • Grade pulses (Table 4.4): 0 not palpable; +1 weak, easily obliterated; +2 difficult to palpate, may be obliterated; +3 easy to palpate (normal); +4 strong, bounding.
  • Compare radial and femoral pulses at least once during infancy to detect coarctation of the aorta and other circulation impairment.

Drug-safety link: before digoxin or rate-limiting drugs, count the apical pulse for 1 full minute and withhold if the pulse is below the ordered rate (e.g., < 60 bpm adolescent, < 90 bpm infant).

6. Respiratory Rate

  • Count respirations when the child is resting or sitting quietly; the rate changes with crying, feeding, and activity, and the most accurate rate is obtained before disturbing the infant (e.g., while the parent holds the child).
  • Count for a full minute for accuracy.
  • Infants breathe primarily diaphragmatically — count abdominal movements; after 1 year of age count thoracic movements.
  • Infants normally display an irregular breathing pattern with short pauses; this may increase when ill.

7. Blood Pressure

When to measure: annually in children 3 years through adolescence and in children with renal/adrenal/cardiovascular disease, hypertension risk factors, emergency/ICU, and high-risk infants.

Method: auscultation is the gold standard under most circumstances; automated/oscillometric devices are acceptable for newborns and infants, where auscultation is difficult, and in intensive care. An elevated reading on an automated device should be repeated using auscultation; oscillometric readings (e.g., Dinamap) can run ~10 mm Hg higher than auscultation, so the two methods are not interchangeable.

Selection of cuff (the most important factor in an accurate BP):

  • Cuff size refers to the inner inflatable bladder, not the cloth covering.
  • Choose a cuff with a bladder width at least 40% of the arm circumference midway between the olecranon and acromion; this usually covers 80–100% of the arm circumference.
  • Too-narrow cuff → falsely high reading. Too-wide cuff → falsely low reading.
  • When the correct size is unavailable, use an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one; do not choose a cuff based on its name ("an infant cuff may be too small for some infants").

Positioning: measure with the cubital fossa at heart level, arm supported, stethoscope bell over the brachial artery proximal and medial to the cubital fossa below the bottom edge of the cuff [p10292–10296].

Interpretation: normal BP is based on gender, age, and height percentiles (tables with 50th, 90th, 95th, 99th percentiles) to avoid misclassifying tall/short children; lesions: BP < 90th percentile normal; 90th–95th prehypertension; ≥ 95th percentile possible hypertension (repeated twice, then staged).

8. Pulse Oximetry (SpO₂)

  • Pulse oximetry (SaO₂) is reliable, noninvasive, and often routine in ill children; the pulse rate on the oximeter should coincide with the apical pulse to ensure the reading is accurate.
  • Decide whether monitoring is continuous or intermittent (with vital signs).

10. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Repeat the checklist at the bedside before disturbing the child where possible.
  • Always document the method (apical/radial; axillary/tympanic/rectal) and the child's activity.
  • Compare every recording to the normal band for that age and to previous readings — an isolated elevated BP may reflect stress, not hypertension.

12. Nursing Care Plan (Vital Signs Focus)

Assessment: a crying 9-month-old in the ward; radial pulse is hard to feel; parent is anxious.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for unstable vital signs / Anxiety (parent) related to the child's illness.

Goals: Vital signs will be measured accurately without false elevation; the parent will understand the readings.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Count respirations first while the parent holds the child, before disturbing — most accurate rate.
  2. Take the apical pulse (child < 2 years), waiting for the rate to slow after stethoscope placement — avoids fear-related tachycardia.
  3. Choose the correct BP cuff (blade ≥ 40% of arm circumference); note that too small a cuff reads falsely high — prevents misdiagnosis.
  4. Reassure the parent by explaining the values against age norms and previous readings — anxiety reduction.
  5. Evaluate: values recorded with method + behavior; provider notified if abnormal.

13. Case Study (Vital Signs)

Newborn, 1 day old, in NICU. Axillary temperature 36.9°C, apical HR 148, RR 52 with abdominal movement, BP by oscillometry 62/40 (left arm). Spo₂ 96% on room air, oximeter pulse 146.

  • Why was axillary temperature used? (Infants < 1 month: axillary recommended; avoiding rectal risks.)
  • Why the apical pulse? (Child < 2 years — radial unreliable.)
  • Why count RR for a full minute observing the abdomen? (Infants breathe diaphragmatically and irregularly.)
  • The BP was taken by oscillometric device; what should happen if it were elevated? (Repeat by auscultation before interpreting — gold standard.)

15. Key Points

  • Order for infants: respirations → pulse → temperature → (BP).
  • Apical pulse for < 2 years always; auscultate apically to 10 years, 1 full minute.
  • Count 1 full minute; infants are diaphragmatic and irregular.
  • BP cuff bladder ≥ 40% of arm circumference; too small = falsely high, too large = falsely low; oversize rather than undersize.
  • Auscultation is the gold standard BP method; repeat elevated automated readings by auscultation.
  • Avoid rectal temperature in immunosuppression/diarrhea/bleeding disorder/rectal surgery.
  • Newborn norms: T 36.5–37.5°C, HR 110–160 (180 crying), RR 30–60, BP 50–75/30–45.
