1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Define communication and therapeutic communication, and list the three essential relationship factors (trust, respect, empathy).
  2. Distinguish verbal from nonverbal communication techniques for interacting with children and families.
  3. Apply the general rules for communicating with children.
  4. Adjust communication to the developmental thought stage of the child (infancy, early childhood, school age, adolescence).
  5. Communicate effectively with parents during the health interview (introduction, privacy, confidentiality, encouragement techniques).
  6. Use play and transition objects as communication tools.
  7. Perform the laboratory and hospital steps of a rapport-building interview, using the chapter checklist.
  8. Write a communication-focused nursing care plan for the child and family.

2. Placement Box

4. Introduction and Definitions

Communication is "the process of sharing information, thoughts, and ideas between two people using a common system of symbols, signs, or behaviors". Infants begin communicating soon after birth when they cry to convey discomfort or hunger.

Therapeutic communication is goal-directed, focused communication with a health purpose: "Client- and family-centered communication increases satisfaction with nursing care and aids in improving knowledge and health care skills". Three factors create and foster effective therapeutic communication: trust, respect, and empathy.

In pediatric nursing the nurse's relationship with the child is frequently mediated by the parent, especially for younger children; assessment therefore gathers input from the child (verbal and nonverbal), the parent, and the nurse's own observations, and for older children the parent becomes a collaborator.

5. The Communication Process and Factors

  • Sender and receiver: both child/parent and nurse.
  • Message: words (verbal) or body language (nonverbal).
  • Feedback: show that the message was received (nodding, reflecting back).
  • Climate: trust, respect, empathy.
  • Barriers: medical jargon, interruptions, noise, adult-directed questions when the child can speak, ignoring the child, lack of privacy.

Safety / Red flag: most parents are laypersons — avoid medical jargon and abbreviations; when a medical term is unavoidable, define it in developmentally appropriate language.

6. Verbal Communication Techniques

Verbal communication is communication through the use of words, written or spoken. General guidelines for appropriate verbal communication include:

  • Use open-ended questions that do not restrict the child's or parents' answers.
  • Redirect the conversation to maintain focus.
  • Use reflection to clarify the parents' feelings.
  • Paraphrase the child's or parents' feelings to demonstrate empathy.
  • Acknowledge emotions.
  • Demonstrate active listening by using the child's or family's own words.
  • Avoid medical jargon; define any necessary medical term.

The nurse uses a variety of verbal techniques to encourage communication; some are useful to pose questions or explore concerns in a less threatening manner; others can be presented as word games, which children often receive well.

7. Nonverbal Communication Techniques

Nonverbal communication, or body language, includes attending to others and active listening; when people feel they are being heard, trust and rapport are established. Guidelines:

  • Relax; maintain an open posture with arms uncrossed.
  • Sit opposite the family and lean forward slightly.
  • Maintain eye contact.
  • Nod the head to demonstrate interest.
  • Note the child's or parent's posture, eye contact, and facial expressions.

TAKE NOTE: people of all ages desire to be listened to without interruption. Active listening may uncover fears that questioning would not reveal. For many children and adults, talking about feelings is difficult, and verbal communication may be more stressful than supportive — then use several nonverbal techniques.

8. Communicating with Parents

8.1 Establishing a setting

  • Appropriate introduction: introduce yourself and ask the name of every family member present and how they wish to be addressed; address adults by title ("Mr.", "Mrs.") unless they specify a preferred name; record the preferred name in the medical record. This conveys respect.
  • Include children in the interaction: ask children their name and age; do not direct all questions to adults when children are old enough to speak for themselves.
  • Privacy and confidentiality: the environment must allow privacy, with distractions (noise, interruptions, TV, mobile phones) kept to a minimum; provide play material for young children during the interview. Inform the family about the limits of confidentiality (e.g., suspected child abuse or suicidal teenager).

8.2 Encouraging parents to talk

  • Whatever the parent sees as a problem should be a concern of the nurse.
  • Careful phrasing with broad, open-ended questions (e.g., "What is Andy eating now?") provides more information than several single-answer questions (e.g., "Is Andy eating what the rest of the family eats?").
  • Take the lead when the parent needs direction, based on an observation: "Joseph seems unhappy today" or "How do you feel when Joseph cries?".
  • Ask about the parent's own coping: "What do you do to relax?" / "What help do you have with the children?".
  • Give positive reinforcement and an opening for questions: "You handle Jamey very well. What kind of experience have you had with babies?".
  • Often a nod or an "uh-huh" keeps parents talking.

8.3 Directing the focus

The nurse directs the focus of the interview while allowing maximum freedom of expression.

9. Communicating with Children: General Rules

Nursing Care Guidelines — Communicating With Children:

  • Allow children time to feel comfortable.
  • Avoid sudden or rapid advances, broad smiles, extended eye contact, and other gestures that may be seen as threatening.
  • Talk to the parent if the child is initially shy.
  • Communicate through transition objects (dolls, puppets, stuffed animals) before questioning a young child directly.
  • Give older children the opportunity to talk without the parents present.
  • Assume a position that is at eye level with the child.
  • Speak in a quiet, unhurried, and confident voice.
  • Speak clearly, be specific, and use simple words and short sentences.
  • State directions and suggestions positively.
  • Offer a choice only when one exists.
  • Be honest with children.
  • Allow children to express their concerns and fears.
  • Use a variety of communication techniques.

10. Communication Related to the Development of Thought Processes

Thought processes progress from sensorimotor → perceptual → concrete → abstract (formal operations). This frames how the nurse communicates at each age.

10.1 Infancy (sensorimotor)

  • Infants use and understand mostly nonverbal communication; they smile and coo when content and cry when distressed (hunger, pain, restraint, loneliness).
  • They become quiet when cuddled, rocked, or gently touched; they are comforted by a soft voice; loud harsh sounds and sudden movements are frightening.
  • Techniques: speak softly, use touch, gentle handling; peek-a-boo is an excellent means of initiating communication with infants.

10.2 Early childhood (toddler–preschooler, egocentric)

  • Children younger than 5 years are egocentric — focus communication on them, tell them what they can do and how they will feel.
  • They are concrete and literal — they may attach literal meaning to phrases like "a little stick in the arm"; use simple, direct language.
  • Allow them to touch and examine articles they will come in contact with (a stethoscope bell feels cold; palpating a neck might tickle).
  • Toddlers communicate with their hands (push away, point, pull someone).

10.3 School-age (concrete)

  • They rely less on what they see, more on what they know; they want explanations and reasons.
  • They are interested in the functional aspect of procedures — show how squeezing the bulb makes the "arrow" move; let them operate the bulb.
  • They have a heightened concern about body integrity, including possessions; encourage them to voice concerns to reduce anxiety.

10.4 Adolescence (formal operations; fluctuates)

  • Adolescents fluctuate between child and adult thinking; they may seek the security of childhood when tensions rise.
  • Interview dilemma: talk with the adolescent alone or with parents? Talking with the adolescent first identifies with the young person; talking with parents first may give insight into family relationships. Give both parties a chance to be included; clarify time limits to avoid appearing to take sides.
  • Two views of a problem often exist (teenager vs parents); providing an open, unbiased atmosphere is therapeutic in itself.

11. Play as a Communication Tool

  • Play is the universal language of children and one of the most important forms of communication.
  • The form and complexity of play give clues about physical, intellectual, and social development.
  • Peek-a-boo initiates communication in infants; puppets are universally effective for communicating with children.
  • Nonverbal communication devices that employ pictures can help children with speech difficulties.

12. Nursing Care Plans (Communication Focus)

Care Plan 1 — Impaired Verbal Communication (child) / Therapeutic communication barrier

Assessment: child hides behind parent, refuses eye contact; parent answers for the child; child uses limited words.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for impaired parent–infant attachment / Anxiety (child or parent) related to hospitalization and unknown environment.

Goals: The child will interact with the nurse using at least one communication technique within the encounter; the child will express fears verbally or nonverbally.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Use a transition object (doll/puppet) and talk to the parent first — reduces threat, builds trust.
  2. Position self at the child's eye level; use a quiet, unhurried voice — reduces perceived threat.
  3. Offer choices only where a choice exists — respects autonomy, decreases power struggles.
  4. Acknowledge emotions and paraphrase the child's feelings — demonstrates empathy.
  5. Evaluate: child speaks, points, or nods; parent reports decreased anxiety.

Care Plan 2 — Deficient Knowledge (parent) / Anxiety (parent)

Assessment: parent uses medical terms incorrectly, repeats questions, appears anxious.

NANDA diagnosis: Deficient knowledge related to the child's condition / Anxiety (parent).

Goals: The parent will describe the condition and the planned care in own words; the parent will verbalize reduced anxiety.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Use broad open-ended questions to uncover concerns ("What worries you most about your child?") — yields more information than yes/no questions.
  2. Avoid jargon; define terms in plain language — parents are laypersons.
  3. Provide privacy and undivided attention — the setting is almost as important as the interview.
  4. Reflect concerns and validate feelings — therapeutic technique that promotes the nurse–family relationship.
  5. Evaluate: parent restates key points; anxiety cues reduced.

14. Procedure — Hospital Step: Bedside Application

  • Repeat checklist items 1–10 with a real family, in the ward.
  • Document: preferred name, chief concern in the parent's words, communication observations, and the family's understanding.
  • Never perform the interview a first time in the ward: the lab step comes first.
  • If the child is old enough, give the older child the opportunity to talk without the parents present.

15. Worked Scenario Examples (Communication)

Scenario A — The literal child. A 4-year-old is told he will get "a little stick in the arm." He becomes terrified that a stick will be pushed into his arm. Correction: say "You will feel a small pinch like a mosquito bite, then it will stop" — simple, direct, honest.

Scenario B — The silent toddler. A 2-year-old won't speak to the nurse. Correction: talk to the parent first, then use a puppet/doll, and let the child handle the stethoscope before use.

Scenario C — The anxious parent. A mother answers in short phrases and wants to finish. Correction: ask a broad open-ended question ("What is Ali eating now?") and give positive reinforcement ("You handle him very well — what experience have you had with babies?").

Scenario D — The adolescent. A 13-year-old gives a different story from the parents. Correction: give both parties a chance, clarify the differing perceptions openly, and do not take sides.

18. Key Points

  • Therapeutic communication requires trust, respect, and empathy.
  • Use broad open-ended questions with parents; redirect, reflect, paraphrase, acknowledge emotions, avoid jargon.

  • Adjust to development: infants = nonverbal + soft voice/touch; toddlers/preschoolers = simple concrete language (literal meaning); school-age = explanations and participation; adolescents = balanced interview with child and parents.
  • Play is the universal language of children.

2. Placement Box

4. Introduction: The Pediatric Health History

The interview is a specific form of goal-directed communication. In the child, the health history is taken from the parent(s) or caregivers, with input from the child when old enough to speak for themselves. An organized approach is most effective.

The complete pediatric health history is outlined in Box 4.4 , which includes:

  1. Identifying information — name, address, telephone, birth date and place, race/ethnic group, sex, religion, date of interview, informant.
  2. Chief complaint (CC) — the major specific reason for seeking health care.
  3. History of present illness (HPI) — all details related to the CC.
  4. Past medical history (PMH) — birth history, previous illnesses/injuries/surgeries, allergies, current medications, immunizations, growth and development, habits.
  5. Review of systems (ROS) — each body system screened.
  6. Family history — relevant hereditary or familial conditions.
  7. Psychosocial history — family structure, habits, school, environment.
  8. Dietary/nutritional history — detailed for the child.

5. The Chief Complaint (CC)

  • The chief complaint is the specific reason for the child's visit; it may be the theme, with the present illness viewed as the description of the problem.
  • Elicit the CC with open-ended, neutral questions: "What seems to be the matter?", "How may I help you?", "Why did you come here today?".
  • Do not use labeling-type questions ("How are you sick?", "What is the problem?") — they limit information.
  • The reason for the visit may not be an illness — it may be a developmental concern, school referral, or procedure.

6. History of Present Illness (HPI)

The HPI is a narrative of the chief complaint from its onset through its progression to the present. Four major components:

  1. Details of onset
  2. A complete interval history
  3. The present status
  4. The reason for seeking help now

The focus is on all factors relevant to the main problem, even if they have disappeared or changed.

Analyzing a symptom

  • Type (character)
  • Location
  • Severity — best determined by how it affects the child's usual behavior; pain that prevents playing, interacting, sleeping, and eating is most often severe; use a rating scale such as the numeric or Wong-Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale
  • Duration — onset, frequency, described in terms of activity/behavior
  • Influencing factors — precipitating, relieving, temporal, positional, and associated events

7. Past Medical History (PMH)

7.1 Birth history

Includes all data concerning:

  1. The mother's health during pregnancy
  2. The labor and delivery
  3. The infant's condition immediately after birth

Because prenatal influences significantly affect a child's physical and emotional development, a thorough birth history is essential. Explain its relevance when parents question it: "I will be asking you some questions about your pregnancy and [child's name]'s birth. Your answers will give me a more complete picture of his overall health".

7.2 Previous illnesses, injuries, and surgeries

  • Begin with a general question ("What other illnesses has your child had?"), then specifically ask about colds, earaches, measles, rubella, chickenpox, mumps, pertussis, diphtheria, tuberculosis, scarlet fever, strep throat, recurrent ear infections, influenza, gastroesophageal reflux, tonsillitis, and allergic manifestations.
  • Ask about injuries that required intervention (falls, poisoning, choking, concussion, fractures, burns), surgeries, procedures, and hospitalizations with dates and diagnoses.

7.3 Allergies

Ask about hay fever, asthma, unusual drug/food/latex reactions, and contact agents. Ask specifically and reliably about drug reactions : name of the drug, the reaction, route, time to reaction, recurrence on rechallenge, and whether it was documented.

Nursing Alert: information about allergic reactions is essential — failure to document a serious reaction places the child at risk if the agent is given.

7.4 Current medications

Ask about vitamins, antipyretics (especially aspirin), antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, supplements, herbs/essential oils, homeopathic medicines. Record name, dose, schedule, duration, reason. Ask parents to bring the containers or call the pharmacy (OTC drugs won't appear on prescriptions).

7.5 Immunizations

A record of all immunizations is essential; list the disease, number of injections, dose, date, and any reaction. Sources: provider record, school record, centralized immunization registry. Screening for contraindications and precautions before every vaccine.

7.6 Growth and development

  • Growth: measurements of height/length and head circumference at birth, patterns on the growth chart, deviations from previous percentiles, family concerns.
  • Development: age of head control, sitting alone, walking, first words, bladder/bowel control, school grade and performance, best friend, interactions with peers and adults. Use specific, detailed questions ("sitting up" can mean different things).

8. Adapting the History to the Child's Age and Situation

  • Infants: emphasize the birth history, detailed feeding history, immunizations, and growth and development.
  • Older children and adolescents: history taking should include the child directly; give older children the opportunity to talk without the parents present.
  • Emergent situations: a focused health history — chief complaint and essentials only.

9. Nursing Care Plan (History/Interview Focus)

Assessment: parent anxious, short answers; child plays in background.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for compromised family coping / Anxiety (parent) related to the health concern.

Goals: The parent will provide a complete, accurate history; anxiety cues will decrease during the interview.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Establish the setting (introduction, privacy, preferred names) before questions — builds trust and reliability.
  2. Begin each section with an open-ended statement ("Tell me about your child's birth") then use fact-finding questions to focus — yields the most complete data.
  3. Explain why birth-history questions are asked for an older child — increases cooperation.
  4. Use the medication containers and the allergy checklist — improves accuracy.
  5. Evaluate: history complete; parent reports feeling heard; no vital data omitted.

10. Case Study (History led to diagnosis)

Hiba, 6 weeks. The mother brought her to the clinic because "she vomits after every feed." CC confirmed with an open-ended question. HPI: projectile vomiting began at 4 weeks, worsening; the mother states the baby is hungry again soon after feeding. PMH birth history: full term, normal delivery, birth weight 3.2 kg.

  • Which CC question was most appropriate? (A neutral open-ended: "What seems to be the matter?")
  • Which part of the HPI is now incomplete? (Analyzing the symptom: type, severity, duration, influencing factors — here the feeding–vomiting relationship)
  • Why does the nurse explain the birth-history questions? (To improve cooperation)

This history pattern (projectile, non-bilious, hungry after feeds) is the classic presentation re-trained in Chapter 19 (Pyloric stenosis).

14. Key Points

  • The pediatric history follows Box 4.4 : identifying info, CC, HPI, PMH (birth/allergies/meds/immunizations/growth), ROS, family, psychosocial, dietary.
  • Elicit the CC with open-ended, neutral questions; never labeling questions.
  • The HPI is a narrative with four components: onset, interval history, present status, and reason for seeking help now.

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Prepare the examination environment (lighting, warmth, privacy, play) and gather the required equipment.
  2. Approach the child according to developmental age and stage (infant, toddler, preschooler, school-age, adolescent).
  3. Apply the Nursing Care Guidelines for performing a pediatric physical examination (nonthreatening order, secure position, ABC in emergencies).
  4. Use the four assessment techniques (inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation) in the correct order for each system.
  5. Sequence the examination head-to-toe with painful/intrusive areas last.
  6. Read the child's readiness signals and apply the refusal techniques.
  7. Perform the lab and hospital steps of a systematic head-to-toe examination using the checklist.

2. Placement Box

Ward: All wards. Each ministerial chapter re-trains a focused examination:

NICU — newborn examination (jaundice: skin/sclerae; RDS: respiratory);

Blood ward — lymph nodes, liver/spleen, joints;

Medical ward — chest auscultation (pneumonia), skin turgor/capillary refill (dehydration);

Surgical ward — abdominal examination (Hirschsprung, pyloric stenosis).

4. Preparing the Physical Examination

Being prepared and organized ensures efficiency; the approach depends on the child's developmental stage.

Environment and equipment:

  • Room well lit and decorated with neutral colors; temperature comfortably warm.
  • Strange, potentially frightening equipment kept out of sight; bring out one piece at a time.
  • Toys/games available; washable toys as distractions.
  • Privacy, especially for school-age children and adolescents.
  • Provide time for play and becoming acquainted.

Gather the equipment: clean gloves, stethoscope, thermometer, sphygmomanometer, tape measure, reflex hammer, penlight, otoscope/ophthalmoscope, tongue depressor, cotton ball, infant or adult scale, stadiometer.

  • Display a confident, matter-of-fact approach; frustration is sensed by child and parent.
  • Keep the room warm; allow the child to remain dressed until the time of the examination.

5. Approaching the Child by Developmental Stage

General rule: approach the child according to developmental age; if several children are seen, begin with the most cooperative child to model the behavior.

Newborns and infants:

  • If the infant is asleep, auscultate the heart, lungs, and abdomen first while the baby is quiet; count HR and RR before undressing.
  • Completely undress down to the diaper; remove the diaper last (genitalia, anus, spine, hips).
  • Examine 1–2 hours before a feeding; have the parent hold the child; parent stays the nurturer, not an assistant of painful procedures.
  • Head-to-toe order; most traumatic (ears, nose, mouth, throat) last; delay the Moro reflex until the end (startle → crying).
  • Warm hands and stethoscope; soft crooning voice, smile, eye contact; bright objects; pacifier if crying.
  • Stranger anxiety is normal in older infants — keep the parent in view.

Toddlers:

  • Remove clothing one item at a time; replace clothing after each area; gown not needed before school age.
  • Use little touch at the beginning; introduce equipment slowly; let the child touch it, take a parent's temperature, put the cuff on a teddy bear.
  • Sit on the caregiver's lap; knee-to-knee for the abdominal examination.
  • Praise cooperation.
  • Use short phrases telling what you'll do, not asking permission. Toddlers are egocentric — don't reference another child's behavior.

Preschoolers:

  • May fear body invasion/mutilation; withdraw from intrusive steps; use simple explanations and reassurance.
  • Let the child "help" (hold stethoscope/penlight); offer real choices; compliment cooperation.
  • Games encourage deep breathing: hold up a finger or penlight and "blow it out".

School-age:

  • Concrete thinking — avoid jargon and double-meaning words: "Let's see how warm you are" instead of "take your temperature".
  • Respect body privacy: keep underpants on under the gown; replace clothing promptly.
  • Commenting on findings is interesting to them — they like to learn how the body works.

Adolescents:

  • Provide privacy while undressing; expose only the area examined; matter-of-fact comments about physical changes; allow questions without the caregiver present; no "dumb questions".

6. General Rules: Performing the Pediatric Examination

Nursing Care Guidelines — Performing Pediatric Physical Examination:

  • Observe readiness signals: talking to the nurse, eye contact, accepting equipment, allowing touch, choosing the examining table over the parent's lap.
  • If not ready: talk to the parent while "ignoring" the child, making complimentary remarks; tell a funny story or do a magic trick; use a hand puppet "friend".
  • If the child refuses: assess the reason (an unduly afraid child may have had a traumatic experience); involve child and parent; avoid prolonged explanations; use a firm, direct approach; perform quickly; gentle restraint by an attendant; minimize stimulation; quiet calm confident voice.
  • Begin nonthreateningly: games (cranial-nerve tests, Simon Says); paper-doll technique — trace the child's outline and use it to demonstrate what will be examined.
  • Involve the child: choices (table vs lap), allow handling equipment, demonstrate on a doll or parent.
  • Sequence: usually head-to-toe with exceptions: alter for age (Table 4.2); examine painful areas last; in an emergency examine ABC (airway, breathing, circulation) first.
  • Reassure throughout (especially pubertal body concerns); discuss findings with the family at the end; praise and reward the child.

7. Assessment Techniques (IPPA)

The four techniques are used in system-specific order. For most systems the order is inspection → palpation → percussion → auscultation; for the abdomen the order is inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation — palpation last because it may alter bowel sounds.

  • Inspection: observation first (color, shape, movement, symmetry). Adequate lighting is necessary.
  • Palpation: superficial then deep; light for most areas; to reduce tickling, place the child's hand over the palpating hand or have the child squeeze the parent's hand.
  • Percussion: tone assessment (resonance/dullness) in chest and abdomen.
  • Auscultation: use the appropriate side of the stethoscope (bell = low frequency e.g., heart sounds; diaphragm = high frequency e.g., breath sounds); in infants auscultate before palpation/percussion of the chest and do it when the infant is quiet.

8. Sequence of the Examination (Head-to-Toe Model)

The examination is recorded following the head-to-toe model even when the actual order is altered for the child.

  1. Vital functions first in an emergency (ABC).
  2. Growth measurements — weight, length/height, head circumference, arm circumference (see Chapter 4).
  3. General appearance — alertness, posture, hygiene, interaction.
  4. Head and neck — fontanelles, suture lines, eyes (red reflex), ears, nose, throat (last, most traumatic), neck lymph nodes.
  5. Chest — inspection (shape, retractions), auscultation of breath and heart sounds (quiet first).
  6. Abdomen — inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation (superficial first).
  7. Genitalia, anus, spine, hips — last; in infants after undressing fully.
  8. Neurologic — reflexes (Moro delayed to the end), motor tone, cranial nerves.

Record observations, not only the abnormal — normal findings are baseline data for the ward notes.

9. Nursing Care Plan (Examination/Preparation Focus)

Assessment: preschooler refuses the examination, hides behind mother, responds to the stethoscope by crying.

NANDA diagnosis: Anxiety (child) related to perceived body invasion/mutilation.

Goals: The child will cooperate with a modified examination; verbal/nonverbal distress will decrease.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Let the child "help" with the stethoscope/penlight and offer choices (lap vs table) — restores a sense of control.
  2. Use the paper-doll technique to demonstrate steps — reduces fear of the unknown.
  3. Explain each step in simple, concrete language ("I want to listen to you breathe") — matches concrete thinking.
  4. Use a game to elicit deep breathing ("blow out the light") — gains cooperation without force.
  5. Preserve privacy (underpants under the gown) and praise cooperation — supports body integrity and positive reinforcement.
  6. Evaluate: the child allows auscultation/palpation; crying stops; parent reports reduced fear.

2. Placement Box

4. Growth Parameters

Measurement of physical growth is a key element in evaluating health status. Physical growth parameters include weight, height (length), skinfold thickness, arm circumference, and head circumference. Values are plotted on percentile charts and compared with the general population.

  • Height and head circumference reflect past nutrition; weight, skinfold thickness, and arm circumference reflect present (current) nutritional status.
  • The most reliable evaluation compares growth measurements over time because growth is a continuous but uneven process.

5. Growth Charts and Interpretation

  • Growth charts use a series of percentile curves to demonstrate the distribution of body measurements.
  • WHO growth standards are used for infants and children 0 to 2 years (reflects growth of predominantly breastfed children); CDC growth charts are used for children 2 years and older.
  • Children whose growth may be questionable:
  • Height and weight percentiles widely disparate (e.g., height 10th and weight 90th with above-average skinfold thickness);
  • Failure to follow the expected growth velocity in height and weight;
  • A sudden increase or decrease in a previously steady pattern (crossing two major percentile lines, especially after 3 years);
  • Short stature in the absence of short parents.

Nursing Alert:

BMI-for-age (sex-specific) categories:

  • Underweight < 5th percentile
  • Healthy weight 5th to < 85th percentile
  • Overweight ≥ 85th and < 95th percentile
  • Obese ≥ 95th percentile

Weight-for-length is used for infants/young children whose length (not height) is measured.

6. Measurement Techniques

6.1 Recumbent length

  • Measure recumbent length in children younger than 24–36 months and in children who cannot stand alone.
  • Use a length board (flat horizontal surface, stationary headboard, smoothly movable footboard at 90°, ruler in mm/1⁄16 in). Never use a tape measure alone — inaccurate and unreliable.
  • Prepare: remove clothing/shoes, loosen/remove diaper, remove hair ornaments on the crown.
  • Two measurers required: assistant holds head in midline with the crown against the headboard (Frankfort vertical plane); lead measurer extends the body (hand on both legs), ensures head against headboard, shoulders/hips not rotated, back not arched, legs not bent, then moves the footboard against the heels of both feet with toes pointing upward.
  • Read to the last completed millimeter; measure at least twice (ideally 3 times) and take the average; record immediately.

6.2 Standing height (stature)

  • Measure height in children 24–36 months and older who can stand alone.
  • Use a stadiometer; never wall charts or "floppy-arm" scale-mounted bars.
  • Remove shoes and heavy outer clothing; undo hairstyles on the crown.
  • Position: back against the vertical surface, occiput, scapulae, buttocks, and heels in contact; erect posture with lordosis minimized, knees fully extended, heels flat, head in the horizontal Frankfort plane.
  • Move the headboard down to the crown (compressing hair); read at eye level (avoid parallax error) to the last completed millimeter; measure ≥ 2 times and average.
  • Diurnal variation: height is less in the afternoon than in the morning due to gravity/spine compression — record the time of day.

6.3 Weight

  • Use an appropriately sized electronic or balance-beam scale: nearest 10 g for infants, 100 g for children.
  • Balance the scale at 0 before weighing; infant scales tend to be more accurate than adult platform scales.
  • Weigh nude when using birth-to-2-year charts; older children in underwear/gown/light clothing; always respect privacy.
  • Child in the center of the scale; keep your hand slightly above the infant/young child to prevent falls.
  • Weigh more than once and use the average; two nurses may weigh independently.

6.4 Head circumference

  • Head circumference reflects brain growth; measure in children up to 36 months and in any child whose head size is questionable.
  • Measure at the greatest frontooccipital circumference — slightly above the eyebrows and pinna of the ears, around the occipital prominence.
  • Use a paper or nonstretchable tape (a cloth tape stretches and gives a falsely small measurement).
  • Measure more than once (head shape and movement affect the reading) to the last completed millimeter.
  • Head circumference is larger than chest circumference in most newborns; they approximate each other around 12 months; later chest exceeds head by about 5–7 cm.

6.5 Arm (midarm) circumference

  • An indirect measure of muscle mass.
  • Place the tape vertically along the posterior upper arm from the acromial process to the olecranon process; half of that length is the midpoint used for the circumference.
  • WHO growth curves are available for triceps skinfold and arm circumference.

6.6 Skinfold thickness

  • A convenient measure of body fat using special calipers (e.g., Lange calipers).
  • Common sites: triceps (most practical), subscapular, suprailiac, abdomen, upper thigh; record the average of at least 2 measurements of one site.

6.7 Quality control

  • Calibrate length boards/stadiometers regularly (ideally daily, at least monthly, and each time they are moved).
  • Measure each child at least twice; measurements should agree within 0.5 cm (ideally 0.3 cm); use the mean.

7. Nursing Care Plan (Growth Assessment Focus)

Assessment: a 6-month-old plotted at the 3rd percentile for weight with a steady drop across two percentile lines; the mother reports normal feeding.

NANDA diagnosis: Imbalanced nutrition: less than body requirements (or Risk for imbalanced nutrition).

Goals: Growth trend will stabilize or improve on re-plotting after 1 month; the family will understand the measurement results.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Re-measure with correct technique (length board, nude weight, calibrated scale) — rules out measurement error before alarming the family.
  2. Plot on the correct WHO/CDC chart by age — prevents misinterpretation (e.g., plotting length vs height on the wrong curve).
  3. Compare current to previous measurements (growth velocity) — growth is continuous but uneven; single values can mislead.
  4. Refer/further assess if BMI/weight-for-length is in an abnormal category.
  5. Evaluate: re-measurement confirms trend; the mother can explain the chart result.

1. Learning Objectives

2. Placement Box

4. Temperature

Definition and norms: core temperature reflects blood flow to the hypothalamus. For rectal temperatures in children, 37.0–37.5°C (98.6–99.5°F) is an acceptable range where heat loss and production are balanced; for neonates a core temperature of 36.5–37.6°C (97.7–99.7°F) is desirable. In the neonate, temperature is monitored for thermoregulation adequacy, not just for fever.

Routes:

  • Axillary — recommended for infants younger than 1 month; nonintrusive, preferred when possible.
  • Tympanic membrane (ear) — takes precedence for children older than 2 years; meta-analysis shows wide variability — do not base a fever-without-focus diagnosis on tympanic readings alone.
  • Temporal artery — acceptable in all age-groups, correlates better with rectal than axillary/tympanic in studies.
  • Rectal — closest to core temperature but invasive; take the temperature of the young infant supine with legs flexed, or prone/side-lying; insert past the anal sphincter no more than 2.5 cm (1 inch); apply water-soluble jelly.

Nursing Alert: avoid the rectal route in the immunosuppressed child and in the child with diarrhea, a bleeding disorder, or a history of rectal surgery.

Nursing Alert: axillary temperature is lower than oral, but the difference varies too widely for a standard conversion — do not add a degree to the axillary reading. In neonates with fever, axillary alone should not be used for clinical decisions.

Order of measurement in infants (before disturbing): count respirations first, take the pulse next, then measure temperature; if the child is disturbed (crying), record the behavior along with the measurement.

5. Pulse (Heart Rate)

  • Assess the heart rate while the child is resting or sleeping; the rate in infants is much faster than in adults and varies with anxiety, fear, or crying.
  • In children younger than 2 years, always assess the apical pulse — the radial pulse is difficult to palpate accurately in this age group. For children younger than 10 years, auscultate the apical pulse with the stethoscope for a full minute; in older children palpate the radial pulse for a full minute.
  • Wait several seconds after placing the stethoscope on the chest for the rate to slow from fear, then count 1 full minute.
  • Count 1 full minute in infants/young children because of possible irregularities.
  • Grade pulses (Table 4.4): 0 not palpable; +1 weak, easily obliterated; +2 difficult to palpate, may be obliterated; +3 easy to palpate (normal); +4 strong, bounding.
  • Compare radial and femoral pulses at least once during infancy to detect coarctation of the aorta and other circulation impairment.

Drug-safety link: before digoxin or rate-limiting drugs, count the apical pulse for 1 full minute and withhold if the pulse is below the ordered rate (e.g., < 60 bpm adolescent, < 90 bpm infant).

6. Respiratory Rate

  • Count respirations when the child is resting or sitting quietly; the rate changes with crying, feeding, and activity, and the most accurate rate is obtained before disturbing the infant (e.g., while the parent holds the child).
  • Count for a full minute for accuracy.
  • Infants breathe primarily diaphragmatically — count abdominal movements; after 1 year of age count thoracic movements.
  • Infants normally display an irregular breathing pattern with short pauses; this may increase when ill.

7. Blood Pressure

When to measure: annually in children 3 years through adolescence and in children with renal/adrenal/cardiovascular disease, hypertension risk factors, emergency/ICU, and high-risk infants.

Method: auscultation is the gold standard under most circumstances; automated/oscillometric devices are acceptable for newborns and infants, where auscultation is difficult, and in intensive care. An elevated reading on an automated device should be repeated using auscultation; oscillometric readings (e.g., Dinamap) can run ~10 mm Hg higher than auscultation, so the two methods are not interchangeable.

Selection of cuff (the most important factor in an accurate BP):

  • Cuff size refers to the inner inflatable bladder, not the cloth covering.
  • Choose a cuff with a bladder width at least 40% of the arm circumference midway between the olecranon and acromion; this usually covers 80–100% of the arm circumference.
  • Too-narrow cuff → falsely high reading. Too-wide cuff → falsely low reading.
  • When the correct size is unavailable, use an oversized cuff rather than an undersized one; do not choose a cuff based on its name ("an infant cuff may be too small for some infants").

Positioning: measure with the cubital fossa at heart level, arm supported, stethoscope bell over the brachial artery proximal and medial to the cubital fossa below the bottom edge of the cuff [p10292–10296].

2. Placement Box

4. The Basic Neurologic Screen

A useful screen before reflex testing:

  • Level of consciousness (LOC): alert, lethargic, obtunded, comatose; observation periods according to age.
  • Pupils: equal, round, reactive to light; note asymmetric or one dilated unresponsive pupil (emergency).
  • Posture and movement: spontaneous activity (none in deep sleep), symmetric movement of all limbs; flaccidity or spasticity.
  • Fontanelles: anterior fontanelle flat, soft, open (closes ~12–18 months); a sunken fontanelle suggests dehydration [Ch 17]; a tense/bulging fontanelle suggests increased intracranial pressure.
  • Cranial nerves: tested at age-appropriate level (e.g., visual red reflex, pupillary response, sucking, facial symmetry).

Safety / Red flag: asymmetric pupils in a child with altered consciousness, a tense bulging fontanelle, or seizures are emergencies — report immediately.

6. Eliciting the Key Reflexes (Technique Notes)

  • Sucking and rooting: place a nipple or nonlatex gloved finger in the mouth (vigorous suck expected — weak suck should be reported); root by stroking the cheek and noting the head turning to the stimulated side and sucking.
  • Moro: sudden jarring or change in equilibrium (e.g., mild head drop with support); the strongest response is seen in the first 2 months; an asymmetric or partial Moro reflex is abnormal (see Section 7).
  • Tonic neck: after 2 months becomes obligatory for the "fencing" posture; an obligatory tonic neck reflex at any age is abnormal.
  • Grasp: firm but gentle pressure on the palm/soles produces flexion.
  • Babinski: stroke the outer sole upward from the heel and across the ball of the foot; toes hyperextend and the hallux dorsiflexes then fans in infants — normal; a positive Babinski is significant when it persists after 1 year, when it reflects pyramidal tract dysfunction.
  • Dance/step: hold the infant so the sole of the foot touches a hard surface — reciprocal flexion/extension simulates walking.

7. Clinical Significance and Abnormal Findings

  • Asymmetric or partial Moro suggests brachial plexus injury, clavicle fracture, or CNS dysfunction; it should be reported.
  • Persistent primitive reflexes are the earliest clue to cerebral palsy: obligatory tonic neck reflex at any age, and non-obligatory or hyperactivity of the Moro, plantar, and palmar grasp reflexes beyond the normal age.
  • Asymmetric tonic neck reflex (evoked by flexing or extending the head) persisting abnormally has diagnostic significance.
  • Absence of tonic neck reflex and asymmetric posturing (except the tonic neck reflex) are reportable findings in the newborn.
  • Exaggerated/hyperactive Moro (e.g., in drug-exposed newborns) and inability to coordinate swallowing should be noted.
  • In a child, a fluctuating Babinski reflex is often observed after seizures.

2. Placement Box

4. Why Children Differ in Fluid Balance

  • Infants have a proportionately greater amount of body water than adults, so their fluid intake requirement per kilogram is greater, and they are at higher risk of fluid loss.
  • Kidney immaturity limits water and electrolyte conservation; insensible losses are relatively larger in infants (larger body surface area per kg).
  • Calorie needs for growth are high, so very young children need 100 kcal/kg/day for the first 10 kg — matching the 100 mL/kg/day figure of the Holliday–Segar method.

  1. 8-kg infant: 8 × 100 = 800 mL/day (≈ 33 mL/hr).
  2. 12-kg toddler: (10 × 100) + (2 × 50) = 1000 + 100 = 1100 mL/day (≈ 46 mL/hr).
  3. 40-kg school child: 1000 + 500 + (20 × 20) = 1000 + 500 + 400 = 1900 mL/day (≈ 79 mL/hr) — plus calories similarly.

7. Intravenous Fluid Therapy in the Hospital

Patients usually requiring IV fluids: severe dehydration, uncontrollable vomiting, inability to drink (extreme fatigue, coma), and severe gastric distention.

  • The IV solution is usually a saline solution with 5% dextrose (D5); sodium bicarbonate may be added because acidosis accompanies severe dehydration.
  • Rapid replacement is contraindicated in hypertonic dehydration because of the risk of water intoxication.
  • Frequency of assessment: monitor the correct concentration, flow rate, infusion site, and intake/output; weigh the child daily — accurate weight is one of the best indicators of fluid status.

7.1 Drop-Rate Calculations (Worked Examples)

Formula: Drops/min = (Total mL × Drop factor) ÷ Minutes

  • Macro drip sets deliver 15–20 gtts/mL; microdrip delivers 60 gtts/mL (so, drop rate in gtts/min = mL/hr).
  • Example (macro 20): 500 mL over 8 hours = 500 × 20 ÷ 480 = ≈ 21 drops/min.
  • Example (microdrip 60): 800 mL over 24 hours = 33 mL/hr → 33 drops/min.
  • Teach: 1000 mL over 24 h with a microdrip = 1000/24 ≈ 42 mL/hr ≈ 42 gtts/min.

Recheck the electric pump by drop counting for 1 minute and verify against the written order; investigate any discrepancy.

8. Monitoring Fluid Status: Urine Output and I/O

  • Minimal acceptable urine output: 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr in children; oliguria is urine output < 1 mL/kg/hr and anuria = no urine.
  • Maintain strict intake/output record and weigh daily; monitor capillary refill, sensorium, skin turgor, oral mucosa, and presence of tears every 4–8 hours.
  • The most reliable indicators of volume status are capillary refill, alterations in sensorium, and urinary output.

9. Electrolytes and Calories at the Bedside

  • Sodium (Na): main cation of the extracellular fluid, 135–145 mmol/L. Low-sodium ORS/fluids are used in maintenance to avoid hypernatremia.
  • Potassium (K): 3.5–5.5 mmol/L; only add K to IV fluids when urine output is adequate; never give K by IV push.
  • Calories: 1 g carbohydrate ≈ 4 kcal; glucose is 3.4 kcal/g. Use the 100/50/20 method for maintenance calories of parenteral support.

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain why pediatric dosing is weight-based and why it differs from adult dosing.
  2. Calculate a safe mg/kg dose, verify it against the recommended range, and convert it to mL of the available preparation.
  3. State when body surface area (BSA) dosing is used and calculate BSA with the Mosteller formula.
  4. Apply the safety "rights," two-identifier verification, and high-alert drug double-checking.
  5. Administer oral and intramuscular medication using atraumatic, aspiration-safe techniques.

2. Placement Box

4. Why Pediatric Dosing Is Different

  • Neonates and preterm infants are especially vulnerable: immature liver enzyme systems, lower plasma protein concentrations, and immature kidney function.
  • Beyond the newborn period, children metabolize many drugs more rapidly than adults, so they may need larger doses per kilogram and/or more frequent administration — important in pain control.
  • Doses are therefore expressed in mg/kg per dose or per day, and some drugs (e.g., chemotherapy) are dosed by BSA in mg/m².
  • BSA vs weight: an infant who is shorter and weighs less has relatively more BSA than expected from the weight.

5. Weight-Based Dose Calculation

General formula:

  • Dose per day = weight (kg) × recommended mg/kg/day
  • Dose per single administration = weight (kg) × recommended mg/kg/dose (or divide the daily dose by the number of doses/day)
  • Volume to give (mL) = ordered dose (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL)

5.1 Worked Examples

Example 1 (classic — bank Q14 style): Child weighs 22 lb; recommend 20–40 mg/kg/day divided every 8 h.

  • Convert: 22 lb ÷ 2.2 = 10 kg.
  • Daily range: 10 × 20 = 200 mg to 10 × 40 = 400 mg/day ✓ (safe range 200–400 mg/day).
  • Per dose (3 doses/day): 67–133 mg per dose.

Example 2 (paracetamol): 8-kg infant; 10–15 mg/kg/dose q4–6h, max 5 doses/day.

  • 8 × 12.5 (midrange) = 100 mg/dose.
  • Available syrup 160 mg/5 mL (32 mg/mL) → 100 ÷ 32 ≈ 3.1 mL per dose (give with an oral syringe in mL only).

Example 3 (antibiotic per day vs per dose): Amoxicillin 40 mg/kg/day divided q8h for a 12-kg child.

  • 12 × 40 = 480 mg/day → 160 mg/dose q8h.
  • Suspension 250 mg/5 mL (50 mg/mL) → 160 ÷ 50 = 3.2 mL q8h.

Teaching: always verify the calculated dose falls within the safe range before giving; if it does not, or if the preparation/route is doubtful, check with the prescriber before proceeding — the nurse is legally liable for the drug they administer.

6. Body Surface Area (BSA) Dosing

  • Used for drugs where metabolic rate matters (e.g., chemotherapy); expressed as mg/m².
  • Mosteller formula: BSA (m²) = √[(height cm × weight kg) ÷ 3600].
  • The West nomogram is a graphic alternative.
  • Worked example: height 90 cm, weight 15 kg → √[(90 × 15) ÷ 3600] = √(0.375) = 0.61 m². A drug ordered at 50 mg/m² = 50 × 0.61 = ≈ 30.6 mg.

7. Medication Safety Before Giving

  • Identify with two identifiers (e.g., name and medical record number or birth date); the parent can verify identity for an infant or nonverbal child.
  • Apply the rights of medication administration (right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, reason) and independently recheck dose calculations.
  • High-alert medications (e.g., antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants, chemotherapy, insulin, epinephrine, opioids, sedation agents) must be double-checked by a second nurse before administration.
  • A misplaced decimal point can cause a 10-fold or greater dosing error.
  • "Look-alike, sound-alike" drug names (e.g., DOBUTamine/DOPamine, predniSONE/prednisoLONE) use Tall Man lettering to emphasize differences.
  • If part of an oral dose is lost by spitting/vomiting, do not automatically repeat — consult the provider; repeat dosing may cause overdose.

8. Oral Administration

  • The oral route is preferred for ease of administration.
  • Measurement: the most accurate device is the plastic disposable oral syringe; the AAP recommends dosing liquid medications in milliliters only, never in teaspoons. Cups (esp. <5 mL) and household spoons are inaccurate; droppers vary with viscosity.
  • Infant technique: hold the infant semireclining; place the tip of the syringe in the pocket between the lower teeth and cheek; give small increments, allowing swallowing between each; never aim straight at the back of the throat (aspiration risk).
  • NURSING TIP: blowing a small puff of air in the face of an infant up to 11 months old frequently elicits a swallow reflex.
  • Do not add medicine to the infant's formula bottle (child may refuse the formula, and the full dose may not be taken).
  • Crushing: only crush when a liquid is unavailable; do not crush enteric-coated or sustained/slow-release products (overdose risk); split only scored tablets; a half dose can be made by dissolving a tablet in 5 mL of water and giving 2.5 mL.
  • Atraumatic tips: mix with a small amount (≈5 mL) of sweet substance; avoid essential foods; give a "chaser"; pinch the nose + straw works by blocking smell; give choices to the child to increase cooperation.

9. Intramuscular (IM) Administration

Volumes (single site): maximum ~1 mL in small children/older infants; small infants ≤ 0.5 mL; larger children approach adult volumes as muscle mass grows.

Recommended site (CDC/WHO/AAP): vastus lateralis for infants:

  • Landmarks: greater trochanter and knee; divide the distance into thirds and inject into the middle third; needle inserted perpendicular to the knee (22–25 gauge, ⅝–1 inch).
  • Ventrogluteal is suitable for children and infants from 2 months.

Technique details:

  • Use a tuberculin syringe (0.01-mL graduations) for volumes <1 mL and low-dose syringes for <0.1 mL.
  • Do not use an air bubble flush when giving <1 mL — it adds extra drug; dead space in the hub is not part of the calibrated dose.
  • Use the Z-track method for irritating drugs (e.g., iron dextran, tetanus toxoid) to prevent tracking into subcutaneous tissue.
  • Needle gauge: smallest that safely delivers (25–30 gauge = least discomfort); larger gauges for viscous drugs.

Ear drops (child ≥ 3 years): pull the pinna upward and backward (bank Q15).

4. Principles of Immunization

  • Vaccines for routine immunizations are among the safest and most reliable drugs available; minor side effects occur, and serious reactions are rare.
  • Vaccines may contain inactive components (preservatives, stabilizers, adjuvants, antibiotics such as neomycin, and culture-medium proteins such as egg) — the child may react to these rather than to the vaccine itself.
  • Types:
  • Live-attenuated (e.g., MMR, varicella, rotavirus, BCG): multiplication of the virus may be enhanced in the immunocompromised → generally not given to severely immunocompromised persons.
  • Inactivated/recombinant (e.g., DTaP, HepB, IPV, Hib, PCV, influenza): cannot replicate; side effects typically local and occur within hours–days.
  • HepB is a recombinant vaccine made by splicing a gene into yeast cultures; a severe yeast hypersensitivity would preclude its use without specialist review.

6. The Cold Chain and Vaccine Storage

  • Proper vaccine storage is critical to vaccine efficacy. If you suspect a vaccine was not maintained at the proper storage temperature, do not use it — an ineffective vaccine does not prevent disease.
  • Standard cold-chain temperature: +2 to +8 °C (as per national cold-chain policy). Protect vaccines from freezing and from light.
  • Placement: store vaccine bottles on a center shelf of the refrigerator, not in the door, where frequent temperature changes on opening can alter potency.
  • Protect light-sensitive vials by wrapping in aluminum foil.
  • Keep the refrigerator temperature log (twice-daily recording) and ensure no vaccine is used after its expiration date.
  • Follow the manufacturer's directions for storage and reconstitution for each vaccine (diluent, mixing, and time limits after reconstitution).

7. Contraindications and Precautions

Contraindications (do not give):

  • Severe febrile illness — the general contraindication for all immunizations.
  • Severe/unknown immunocompromise — for live vaccines (MMR, varicella, rotavirus, BCG).
  • Pregnancy — for MMR; breastfeeding is not a contraindication for any vaccine.
  • Recent passive immunity: MMR and varicella postponed ≥3 months after immunoglobulin/blood transfusions (except washed red cells).
  • Anaphylaxis to a previous vaccine or a component (e.g., anaphylaxis to neomycin, gelatin) — proper contraindication.

NOT contraindications (common misconceptions):

  • Minor illnesses such as the common cold are not a contraindication.
  • Egg sensitivity is not a contraindication for MMR or inactivated influenza.
  • Family history of seizures/SIDS, penicillin allergy, and neomycin contact dermatitis are not contraindications.
  • A precaution is a condition that might increase the risk; give the vaccine only if benefit outweighs risk (e.g., a moderate acute illness with fever).

2. Placement Box

4. Hand Hygiene and Standard Precautions

  • Each day about 1 in 31 patients acquires at least one hospital-acquired infection (HAI); HAIs are preventable with meticulous hand washing, cleaning, and disposal.
  • Hand hygiene is the single most important practice to reduce transmission of infectious diseases in the health care setting; includes washing with soap and water and use of alcohol-based hand disinfectants.
  • Standard Precautions synthesize universal precautions (blood/body fluids) and body-substance isolation:
  • Use barrier PPE — gloves, goggles, gowns, and masks — for contact with blood, all body fluids/secretions/excretions (except sweat), nonintact skin, and mucous membranes.
  • Standard Precautions apply to all patients because unrecognized sources of infection exist.
  • Respiratory Hygiene/Cough Etiquette and safe injection practices are part of Standard Precautions: cover mouth/nose when coughing, mask coughing persons, 3 feet of space, tissues; use safety-engineered sharps.
  • Glove use: gloves do not provide complete protection — wash hands thoroughly after removing gloves; disposable-diaper plastic lining is a sufficient barrier, so gloves are adequate for routine diaper changes (add gown for explosive/liquid stools).
  • Sharps: dispose of all needles (uncapped, unbroken) in rigid, puncture-resistant containers near the point of use, placed out of children's reach; prefer needleless systems.
  • MRSA transmission is mainly patient-to-patient via the hands of health care providers; hand washing is the most critical infection-control practice.

6. Preparing the Child for Isolation (Pediatric Approach)

Admission to an isolation room increases all the stressors of hospitalization: further separation, loss of control, sensory deprivation, and strangers.

  • Preschoolers cannot understand cause-and-effect; they view isolation as punishment. Use a simple story: "You need to be in this special room to help you get better; the infection made you sick and you could not help that".
  • Prepare the child for what they will see, hear, and feel: show the mask, gloves, and gown and let the child "dress up" in them; playing with PPE lessens fear of staff entering.
  • Staff should introduce themselves and let the child see their faces before donning masks, so the child associates them with familiar people.
  • Provide appropriate play activities when the child's condition improves; keep explanations developmentally matched.

7. Principles of Atraumatic Care

Atraumatic care is therapeutic care that minimizes or eliminates the psychological and physical distress experienced by children and their families in the health care system (premise: "do no harm").

Box 30.1 — Principles :

  • Prevent or minimize physical stressors: pain, discomfort, immobility, sleep deprivation, inability to eat/drink, changes in elimination.
  • Avoid or reduce intrusive/painful procedures (multiple punctures, injections, urethral catheterization).
  • Reduce other physical distress: noise, smells, shivering, nausea, sleeplessness, restraints, skin trauma.
  • Control pain by frequent assessment plus pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions.
  • Prevent or minimize parent–child separation.
  • Promote family-centered care — treat the family as the patient; use core primary nursing.
  • Follow research on whether parents and children prefer to be together.
  • Promote a sense of control (tell the child what will happen, give choices, use distraction).

8. Documentation and Handover

  • Document objectively and immediately: admission assessment, weight and head circumference, vital signs, I/O (including ORS), medication doses (route, site, lot), isolation precautions, and "reflexes observed, fontanelle status" where relevant [Ch 2, 6, 7].
  • Weight daily — one of the best indicators of fluid status in children.
  • Use the structured SBAR handover: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation — especially during ward shift changes and when transferring an isolated child.
  • Legal note: the nurse is accountable for what they document and administer; record the exact time of medications and observations, and report any discrepancy with the written order.

2. Placement Box

Ward: NICU — one of the two neonatal topics. Jaundice is the most common newborn problem requiring ward admission, phototherapy, and (rarely) exchange transfusion.

Foundation skills reused: CH5 vital signs, CH6 neurologic/reflex assessment, CH7 fluid calculations, CH8 medication safety, CH10 core ward skills.

4. Definition and Pathophysiology

  • Hyperbilirubinemia is an excessive level of accumulated bilirubin in the blood, characterized by jaundice (icterus) — a yellowish discoloration of the skin and other organs.
  • Bilirubin is a breakdown product of hemoglobin from RBC destruction. When RBCs are destroyed, hemoglobin splits into heme and globin; the heme portion is converted to unconjugated bilirubin (insoluble, bound to albumin).
  • In the liver, bilirubin is detached from albumin and conjugated with glucuronic acid (enzyme: glucuronyl transferase) to a water-soluble product that is excreted in bile; bacteria reduce it to urobilinogen, the pigment giving stool its color.
  • When developmental limitations or disease interfere with this balance, bilirubin accumulates in the tissues → jaundice.
  • Causes: physiologic (prematurity), breastfeeding/breast-milk association, excess production (hemolysis, bruises), disturbed liver conjugation/excretion, certain diseases (G6PD deficiency, hypothyroidism, galactosemia, infant of diabetic mother), and genetic predisposition.

6. Assessment and Diagnosis

  • Visual assessment is NOT reliable to judge severity — jaundice is estimated by transcutaneous bilirubinometry (TcB) or total serum bilirubin (TSB); visual/transcutaneous estimates are invalid once phototherapy begins.
  • Risk factors for pathologic hyperbilirubinemia include jaundice with onset within 24 hours, rapidly rising levels, maternal blood group O, positive Coombs test, previous sibling with hyperbilirubinemia, and prematurity/illness.
  • Use the hour-specific bilirubin nomogram to determine the risk zone and the phototherapy/exchange thresholds for infants ≥35 weeks.
  • ABO incompatibility: treat with early detection + phototherapy; diagnosis may be hard because the direct Coombs test can be negative or weakly reactive; consider when jaundice appears within 24 hours, bilirubin rises, with RBC spherocytosis.

7. Complications: Bilirubin Encephalopathy and Kernicterus

  • Unconjugated bilirubin is highly toxic to neurons; severe hyperbilirubinemia risks bilirubin encephalopathy (early, varying, reversible symptoms) and kernicterus — yellow staining and necrosis of brain cells with chronic permanent damage.
  • Early signs: lethargy, poor feeding, hypotonia, high-pitched cry, temperature instability (some preterm infants may be asymptomatic).
  • Later signs: athetoid cerebral palsy, extrapyramidal symptoms, opisthotonos, dental enamel hyperplasia, motor delay, seizures, and sensorineural hearing loss — long-term cognitive, motor, and behavioral effects.
  • Safe practice: serum bilirubin alone does not predict CNS injury; risk and neurologic signs guide action.

8. Medical and Nursing Management

8.1 Phototherapy

  • Place the infant under the light source exposing as much skin as possible; position and reposition frequently to expose all body surfaces.
  • Monitor serum bilirubin every 6–12 hours after initiation (visual/TcB no longer valid).
  • Eye protection: opaque mask sized correctly, covering eyes completely without occluding the nares; close the eyelids before applying (corneal excoriation); check eyes every 4–6 hours for discharge/pressure/irritation; remove shields during feeding for visual/sensory stimulation.
  • Temperature: monitor for temperature instability — phototherapy may raise body temperature; maintain manufacturer-specified lamp distance (halogen lights too close cause burns); keep the infant flexed with rolled blankets.
  • Side effects: loose greenish stools (accelerated bilirubin excretion), transient rashes, mild hyperthermia, increased metabolic rate, priapism, rarely dehydration/hypocalcemia; meticulous perianal skin care; add fluids/feedings to compensate for insensible and stool losses; do not use oily lotions (increased tanning).
  • Bronze-baby syndrome: grayish-brown skin/serum/urine, usually with elevated conjugated bilirubin + cholestasis; resolves after discontinuing phototherapy.
  • Rebound effect: a transient rise in bilirubin after phototherapy stops — usually resolves without resuming therapy.
  • Charting must include: start/stop times, eye shielding, phototherapy unit type, number of lamps, lamp-to-infant distance, incubator vs open bassinet use, light intensity (µW), and observed side effects.
  • Family support: reassure parents the infant is warm and comfortable; remove shields and turn off lights during visits to facilitate attachment and eye contact.

8.2 Exchange Transfusion

  • Indications: rapidly rising bilirubin and hemolysis despite aggressive phototherapy; also hydrops fetalis or signs of cardiac failure (immediate procedure).
  • Procedure: sterile surgical technique; catheter inserted into the umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava; 5–10 mL blood withdrawn over 15–20 s and an equal volume of donor blood infused; donor blood typed/cross-matched to the mother's serum; usually double the infant's blood volume (~85 mL/kg), replacing ~85% of circulating blood.
  • Nursing during the procedure: infant stays NPO with a peripheral dextrose/electrolyte infusion; document volumes withdrawn/infused, times, and cumulative total; monitor vital signs continuously, correlate with blood removal/infusion; stop temporarily for cardiac/respiratory problems and resume when stable; observe for transfusion reaction (temperature instability, hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, rash); maintain thermoregulation, glucose, and fluid balance.
  • ABO incompatibility rarely needs exchange transfusion unless phototherapy fails.
  • Family support: parents often feel guilty about "causing" the incompatibility — reassure and praise their prenatal care.

8.3 Prevention and Adjunctive Therapy

  • Prevent Rh sensitization with Rh immune globulin (RhIg) given to the Rh-negative mother (prenatal/postpartum).
  • IVIG and tin-mesoporphyrin (heme oxygenase inhibitor) are adjuncts used with phototherapy per protocol.

4. Definition and Risk Factors

RDS is a surfactant deficiency disorder with physiologic immaturity of the thorax; also called hyaline membrane disease. It occurs almost exclusively in preterm infants but is also associated with multifetal pregnancies, infants of diabetic mothers, cesarean delivery, cold stress, asphyxia, and a family history of RDS.

Non-pulmonary causes of similar distress: sepsis, cardiac defects, cold exposure, airway obstruction (atresia), IVH, hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, acute blood loss, and drugs; neonatal pneumonia may occur alone or complicate RDS.

5. Pathophysiology

  • The final unfolding of alveolar septa occurs in the last trimester; preterm infants are born with underdeveloped, partly uninflatable alveoli; the chest wall is highly compliant (cartilage) and the diaphragm fatigues easily.
  • Surfactant — a surface-active phospholipid secreted by type II alveolar cells — is first produced at ~24 weeks but the type II cells do not mature until ~36 weeks.
  • Surfactant reduces the surface tension of alveolar fluid, allowing uniform inflation at low pressure. Deficiency causes unequal inflation and collapse of alveoli on expiration (atelectasis); each breath may require 60–75 cm H₂O negative pressure, exhausting the infant.
  • Hypoxemia + increased pulmonary vascular pressure cause transudation of fluid into alveoli; necrotic cells + fibrin form the hyaline membrane that lines the alveoli and reduces compliance.
  • Atelectasis → increased pulmonary vascular resistance → right-to-left shunting through the ductus arteriosus and foramen ovale; hypoxemia and hypercapnia → pulmonary vasospasm, worsening PVR.
  • Prolonged hypoxemia causes anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acid → metabolic acidosis; the atelectatic lung cannot clear CO₂ → respiratory acidosis; low pH worsens vasoconstriction.
  • Additional contributors: pulmonary edema (renal immaturity, high fluid intake, PDA, low colloid osmotic pressure, oxygen toxicity), pulmonary interstitial emphysema, and neurologic immaturity (weak gag/cough).

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Distress may be acute or develop over hours. After apparently normal breathing at birth, respirations gradually exceed 60/min with retractions (suprasternal, substernal, supracostal, subcostal, intercostal) from the compliant chest wall.
  • Within hours breathing becomes labored with the rate rising to 80–120/min; infants increase rate rather than depth; fine inspiratory crackles and an audible expiratory grunt (a useful mechanism raising end-expiratory pressure) appear.
  • Nasal flaring accompanies tachypnea, grunting, and retractions.
  • Central cyanosis (bluish oral mucous membranes and generalized cyanosis) is a late and serious sign; use pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases instead of relying on color.
  • Severe RDS → shock-like state (diminished cardiac inflow, low blood pressure). ELBW/VLBW infants may have severe RDS at birth.
  • Assessment grading: the Silverman–Anderson scale grades upper chest/lower chest retractions, xiphoid retractions, nares dilation, and expiratory grunt from 0–2 each.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Laboratory data are nonspecific – hypoxemia, hypercapnia, acidosis; test blood glucose (hypoglycemia), blood gases/pH (acidosis), and PaO₂ (hypoxia); pulse oximetry is an important component.
  • Chest X-ray: (1) a diffuse ground-glass pattern over both lungs (alveolar atelectasis) and (2) air bronchograms (dilated air-filled bronchioles). Distinguish RDS from pneumonia.
  • Antenatal lung maturity tests: L/S ratio (lecithin/sphingomyelin) — an L/S ratio of 2:1 in nondiabetic mothers predicts no significant RDS risk; PG appears in amniotic fluid at ~36 weeks; TDx FLM ≥55 is predictive of maturity; lamellar body counts; "shake"/bubble and tap tests.
  • Antenatal glucocorticoids enhance fetal lung maturity, especially with postnatal surfactant.

8. Therapeutic Management

Six supportive measures are crucial:

  1. Maintain adequate ventilation and oxygenation — CPAP, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), or mechanical ventilation.
  2. Maintain acid–base balance.
  3. Maintain a neutral thermal environment.
  4. Maintain adequate tissue perfusion and oxygenation.
  5. Prevent hypotension.
  6. Maintain adequate hydration and electrolyte status.

Nipple and gavage feeding are avoided when the respiratory rate is markedly increased (aspiration hazard).

  • CPAP delivers 3–8 cm H₂O positive pressure via nasal prongs/face mask, preventing alveolar collapse and increasing diffusion time; the infant breathes spontaneously.
  • IMV/PEEP: mechanical breaths at a preset rate with positive end-expiratory pressure to prevent alveolar collapse; PIP is the peak inspiratory pressure; MAP (mean airway pressure) is the major determinant of oxygenation in severe RDS.
  • SIMV synchronizes ventilator breaths with the infant's own breathing.
  • Complications to prevent/monitor: those of positive-pressure ventilation, plus PDA/congestive heart failure, retinopathy of prematurity, IVH, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, NEC, and neurologic sequelae.

8.1 Surfactant Replacement

  • Exogenous surfactant (natural porcine/bovine or synthetic; e.g., beractant) is given via the endotracheal tube directly into the trachea; single vs multiple doses per protocol.
  • Nursing responsibilities: assist delivery, collect/monitor arterial blood gases, scrupulously monitor oxygenation and tolerance; after absorption there is usually a rise in compliance requiring ventilator adjustment (lower MAP) to prevent overinflation/hyperoxemia; delay suctioning about an hour (per product/protocol) to allow maximum effect.
  • Infant may receive surfactant shortly after birth then be placed on nasal CPAP or HFNC.

4. Definition and Genetics

  • Thalassemia is the term for inherited blood disorders with a deficiency in the rate of production of specific globin chains in hemoglobin; the name derives from the Greek thalassa ("sea"), referring to Mediterranean populations.
  • Beta thalassemia is the most common form, classified as:
  • Thalassemia minor/trait — heterozygous, usually mild microcytic anemia
  • Thalassemia intermedia — moderate–severe anemia with splenomegaly
  • Thalassemia major (Cooley anemia) — homozygous, severe anemia not compatible with life without transfusion support.
  • Mode of transmission: autosomal recessive; when both parents carry the trait, each pregnancy carries a 25% chance of a child with thalassemia major.

5. Pathophysiology

  • Normal HbA is composed of two α and two β polypeptide chains. In beta thalassemia there is a partial or complete deficiency in β-chain synthesis, with compensatory excess of α-chains and continued γ-chain production → unstable, defective hemoglobin.
  • The unstable polypeptide disintegrates, damaging RBCs and causing severe hemolytic anemia; the bone marrow overproduces erythrocytes unless suppressed by transfusions.
  • Excess iron from transfusions and rapid destruction of defective cells is stored in organs (hemosiderosis).

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Before diagnosis: anemia, pallor, unexplained fever, poor feeding, enlarged spleen or liver.
  • Progressive anemia: signs of chronic hypoxia, headache, bone pain, decreased exercise tolerance, listlessness, anorexia.
  • Other features: small stature, delayed sexual maturation, bronzed/freckled complexion (without chelation).
  • Bone changes (untreated, older children): enlarged head, frontal/parietal bossing, prominent malar eminences, flat nasal bridge, enlarged maxilla, protruding upper central incisors/malocclusion, generalized osteoporosis.
  • Onset in major may be insidious, recognized in late infancy or early toddlerhood.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • CBC/smear: microcytosis, hypochromia, anisocytosis, poikilocytosis, target cells, basophilic stippling; low Hgb and Hct in severe anemia.
  • Hemoglobin electrophoresis confirms the diagnosis and distinguishes the type by analyzing the quantity/kind of Hb variants.
  • Screen the whole family for the trait; refer for genetic counseling; prenatal diagnosis by amniocentesis or fetal blood sampling is available.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • Transfusions are the foundation of management; goal: maintain Hgb at approximately 9.5 g/dL, possibly requiring transfusion every 2–5 weeks. Benefits: normal activity, decreased cardiomegaly/hepatosplenomegaly, fewer bone changes, near-normal growth, fewer infections.
  • Iron chelation for hemosiderosis/hemochromatosis (the body cannot eliminate excess iron): deferoxamine (parenteral/subcutaneous infusions), deferasirox (oral), and deferiprone (oral).
  • Splenectomy may be needed for severe splenomegaly/increased transfusion requirements; major complication = severe overwhelming infection — children usually receive prophylactic antibiotics for years plus pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccines.
  • NURSING TIP: the family must notify health personnel of any fever ≥38.5 °C (101.3 °F) because of sepsis risk from asplenia.
  • Cure: allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) — about 80–97% complication-free survival in children <14 years without excessive iron stores/hepatomegaly; gene-therapy approaches are under study.
  • Prognosis: with transfusions + early chelation, survival into adulthood; common causes of death are heart disease, liver cirrhosis, postsplenectomy sepsis, and multiorgan failure from hemochromatosis.

  6. Evaluate: Hgb maintained near 9.5 g/dL; no sepsis/transfusion reactions; chelation doses documented; family states the fever rule.

4. Definition and Epidemiology

  • Leukemia is a broad term for malignant diseases of the bone marrow, blood, and lymphatic system. In healthy children the marrow makes blood stem cells that mature into lymphoid or myeloid cells; in acute leukemias, immature nonfunctional cells predominate.
  • The two most common types in children are acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML).
  • ALL is the most common form of childhood cancer, ~3,000 new cases annually in the U.S.; more common in boys (10% more), more common in Hispanics; peak age of onset between 3 and 5 years.
  • Risk factors: prenatal exposure to x-rays, previous treatment with chemotherapy, and genetic conditions (Down, Bloom, and Fanconi syndromes; Li-Fraumeni); some ALL begins prenatally.

5. Pathophysiology → Clinical Manifestations

  • Signs reflect infiltration of the bone marrow by nonfunctional leukemic cells (blasts). The three main consequences of marrow infiltration:
  1. Anemia from decreased RBCs;
  2. Infection from neutropenia;
  3. Bleeding from decreased platelets.
  • About half of patients have an elevated WBC count at presentation (>20,000/mm³).
  • Common signs: hepatosplenomegaly (75%), splenomegaly alone (24%), fever (63%), lymphadenopathy (60%), bleeding (petechiae/purpura, 20%), bone pain (12%).
  • Onset ranges from acute to insidious; a "cold that will not go away," pallor, listlessness, irritability, fever, anorexia, weight loss, bruising without cause, and continued bone/joint pain should arouse suspicion.
  • Two important extramedullary sanctuaries — sites where leukemic cells hide from systemic therapy: the CNS and the testes; they require specific therapy.

6. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Suspected from history, physical signs, and a peripheral blood smear containing leukemic blasts, frequently with low counts.
  • Work-up: thorough history and physical exam; laboratory tests (CBC with differential, blood chemistries); bone marrow aspirate or biopsy with cytogenetic analysis and immunophenotyping.
  • A chest x-ray may detect a mediastinal mass.
  • Definitive diagnosis is based on analysis of the bone marrow sample — typically a monotonous infiltrate of blast cells.
  • After confirmation, a lumbar puncture checks for CNS involvement.
  • Prognostic (risk) factors including age at diagnosis (favorable 1–10 years), WBC count at diagnosis (favorable ≤50,000/mm³), CNS/testicular involvement, Down syndrome, sex, cytogenetics, and response to initial treatment (minimal residual disease, MRD).
  • NURSING ALERT: children vaccinated 2 weeks before or during chemotherapy are considered unimmunized; revaccinate or give live-virus vaccines only 6 months after chemotherapy stops, following institutional guidelines.

7. Therapeutic Management

  • Long-term disease-free survival now approaches 90% at major centers; treatment is risk-based (high-risk children get the most intensive therapy).
  • Three phases:
  1. Remission induction — begins almost immediately after diagnosis, lasts 4–6 weeks. Drugs: dexamethasone or prednisone, vincristine, L-asparaginase, with or without an anthracycline. Complete remission (CR) = <5% blasts in the marrow and no detectable extramedullary leukemia.
  2. Consolidation/intensification — given after CR (all children need post-induction therapy); the very intensive Berlin–Frankfurt–Münster (BFM) backbone uses cyclophosphamide, cytarabine, mercaptopurine, then intermittent maintenance with high-dose methotrexate (with or without leucovorin rescue) and delayed intensification.
  3. Maintenance (continuation) — daily mercaptopurine, weekly low-dose methotrexate, plus vincristine/corticosteroid and continued intrathecal therapy; adherence <90% increases relapse risk.
  • CNS-directed therapy — because leukemic cells in the CNS are protected from systemic drugs by the blood–brain barrier, all children receive CNS prophylaxis: intrathecal chemotherapy (methotrexate alone or with cytarabine + hydrocortisone) plus CNS-directed systemic therapy (dexamethasone, high-dose methotrexate with leucovorin rescue); cranial radiation for the highest-risk children.
  • Four standard treatment modalities: chemotherapy, radiation therapy, chemotherapy with stem cell transplant, and targeted therapy (e.g., TKIs, monoclonal antibodies, proteasome inhibitors).
  • Supportive therapy (essential in the period immediately after remission when myelosuppression leaves the body defenseless): transfusion support and antibacterial/antifungal agents.
  • Late effects & survivorship: long-term follow-up per the Children's Oncology Group (COG) guidelines; nurses help families access survivorship and transition care.

Assessment: 7-year-old boy, newly diagnosed ALL, WBC 85,000/mm³, Hgb 6.0 g/dL, platelets 60,000/mm³; hepatomegaly; starting induction tomorrow.

  • Risk for injury / bleeding related to thrombocytopenia.
  • Risk for imbalanced fluid volume related to tumor lysis syndrome.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Neutropenic precautions: hand washing, frequent bathing, no shared utensils, avoid crowds/ill contacts; monitor for fever, chills, cough, sore throat and report immediately — prevents/early-detects infection.
  2. Skin and mouth care: keep skin/perianal area clean with mild lotion; frequent mouth care with saline rinses; check for ulcers and gum swelling — prevents mucositis and skin entry of organisms.

  8. Evaluate: afebrile; no infection/bleeding; chemistry normal; family lists fever-rule and precautions.

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Explain the inheritance (X-linked recessive) of hemophilia and differentiate hemophilia A (factor VIII), hemophilia B (factor IX), and von Willebrand disease.
  2. Recognize the clinical manifestations (Box: prolonged bleeding, hemarthrosis, bruising, hematomas, hematuria) and severe complications (airway obstruction, intracranial hemorrhage).
  3. Describe the therapy: factor VIII/IX replacement, DDAVP in mild forms, prophylaxis vs on-demand treatment, RICE for acute bleeds.
  4. Provide nursing care: prevent bleeding, implement RICE, avoid aspirin/IM injections, teach home therapy and self-administration, genetic counseling, and school/activity planning.

2. Placement Box

Ward: Blood ward — third hematology topic. Hemophilia demonstrates precise medication-safety and intravenous skill reuse (CH8), transfusion principles (CH13), and family chronic-care communication (CH1).

4. Definition and Genetics

  • Hemophilia is a group of bleeding disorders from congenital deficiency, dysfunction, or absence of specific coagulation proteins (factors).
  • About 80% of cases are X-linked recessive. The two most common forms are:
  • Hemophilia A (classic) — factor VIII deficiency; prevalence ~1 in 5,000 live births.
  • Hemophilia B (Christmas disease) — factor IX deficiency; ~1 in 20,000–30,000 live births.
  • Von Willebrand disease (vWD) — hereditary bleeding disorder from deficiency/abnormality of the von Willebrand factor (vWF) protein. Factor VIII deficiency accounts for 80–85% of all hemophilia cases.

5. Pathophysiology

  • The basic defect of hemophilia A is deficiency of factor VIII (anti-hemophilic factor [AHF]), produced by the liver and needed for thromboplastin formation in phase I of coagulation. The less factor VIII in the blood, the more severe the disease.
  • Individuals have intact vascular influence and platelets, so they bleed longer, not faster.
  • Three clinical grades (severe, moderate, mild) by severity of factor deficiency; 60–70% of children have the severe form. With severe deficiency, hemorrhage may be spontaneous or from minor trauma (e.g., post-circumcision); with milder forms, bleeding may only be noticed when the child begins to walk.
  • Hemarthrosis (bleeding into joints — knees, elbows, ankles) is the most frequent form of internal bleeding; repeated bleeds cause bone changes and crippling deformities. Early signs of a joint bleed: stiffness, tingling, or achiness, then warmth, redness, swelling, severe pain, and loss of movement.
  • Serious sites: neck/mouth/thorax (airway obstruction), intracranial hemorrhage (a major cause of death), GI bleeding (anemia), retroperitoneal hemorrhage, and spinal hematoma (paralysis).

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Prolonged bleeding anywhere from or in the body.
  • Hemorrhage from any trauma: loss of deciduous teeth, circumcision, cuts, epistaxis, injections.
  • Excessive bruising from even slight injury; subcutaneous and IM hemorrhages.
  • Hemarthrosis (knees, ankles, elbows); hematomas (pain, swelling, limited motion).
  • Spontaneous hematuria.
  • Maintain a high index of suspicion for internal bleeding with: headache, slurred speech, loss of consciousness (cerebral) and black tarry stools (GI).

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Diagnosis from the history of bleeding episodes, evidence of X-linked inheritance (only ~1/3 are new mutations), and laboratory findings.
  • Specific tests: factor VIII and factor IX assays (performed in specialized laboratories).
  • General screening test depending on specific factors: the partial thromboplastin time (PTT).
  • DNA (carrier) testing allows detection as early as classic hemophilia and is important for families where female offspring may have inherited the trait.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • Primary therapy = replacement of the missing clotting factor. Products: factor VIII concentrates (recombinant by genetic engineering, or plasma-derived), reconstituted with sterile water immediately before use.
  • DDAVP (1-deamino-8-d-arginine vasopressin) — synthetic vasopressin that increases plasma factor VIII activity; treatment of choice in mild hemophilia and vWD type I if the child shows an appropriate response (expect a 3–4-fold rise). Not effective in severe hemophilia A, severe vWD, or hemophilia B.
  • Corticosteroids for hematuria, acute hemarthrosis, and chronic synovitis.
  • Avoid aspirin and traditional NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) because they inhibit platelet function → GI bleeding; selective COX-2 inhibitors may be suitable for hemophilic arthropathy.
  • ε-aminocaproic acid (Amicar) prevents clot destruction; limited to mouth trauma or surgery, with factor concentrate given first.
  • Exercise and physical therapy within reasonable limits strengthen muscles around joints and may reduce spontaneous bleeds.
  • Home treatment: most children are treated at home because early treatment gives faster recovery and fewer complications; family is taught venipuncture and AHF administration for children older than 2–3 years; the child learns self-administration at 8–12 years.
  • Prophylaxis: periodic factor replacement for severe hemophilia to prevent arthropathy and spontaneous life-threatening bleeds; primary prophylaxis (regular factor VIII infusion before joint damage) vs secondary prophylaxis (after first joint bleed); infusions every other day to 3 times weekly.
  • Prognosis: no cure, but symptoms controlled and deformities minimized; today's children using recombinant products are at virtually no risk of HIV from treatment (90% of severe hemophiliacs treated 1979–1985 seroconverted to HIV-positive before modern purification). Gene therapy is under development.

9. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 5-year-old boy with severe hemophilia A presents with a swollen, warm right knee after a fall; earlier he had a "stiff feeling" in the joint.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Risk for injury (bleeding episodes) related to factor VIII deficiency.
  • Impaired physical mobility related to hemarthrosis and joint pain.
  • Deficient knowledge (family) related to home factor-replacement and RICE.

Goals: Bleed treated early; joint function preserved; family demonstrates home care; no aspirin/IM injuries.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. RICE: rest, ice, compression, elevation together with factor replacement (cold packs kept in the freezer for emergencies); these measures do not replace factor therapy.
  2. Recognize internal bleeding early: headache, slurred speech, unconsciousness, black tarry stools, unusual swelling/pain — minimizes serious complications.
  3. Prevent bleeding: soft-bristle or water-irrigation dental care, electric shaver (no safety razors), medical identification, supervised play with protective equipment; avoid contact sports (non-contact: swimming, walking, jogging, tennis, bowing, golf, yoga).
  4. Medication precautions: no aspirin or aspirin-containing compounds; acetaminophen is a suitable substitute; avoid IM injections — use subcutaneous or IV routes; prefer venipuncture (less bleeding than finger/heel).
  5. Immobilize and elevate the affected joint during a bleed; institute active range-of-motion after the acute phase; monitor weight to reduce joint strain.
  6. Teach home therapy: reconstitute factor with sterile water immediately before use; family performs venipuncture/AHF infusion; child self-administers at 8–12 years.
  7. Genetic counseling ASAP (support the mother; DNA carrier testing available); teach about safe sexual practices for those exposed before 1990 purification.
  8. Evaluate: bleeding stopped; joint ROM preserved; family performs RICE + factor infusion; school and play plan in place.

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. Define pneumonia and classify it by etiologic agent (viral, bacterial, mycoplasmal/atypical, aspiration) and by age-group organisms.
  2. Recognize the clinical manifestations (Box: fever, cough, tachypnea, crackles/rhonchi, dullness, retractions, nasal flaring, cyanosis, behavior and GI changes).
  3. Describe the therapeutic plan (antibiotics, rest, fluids, antipyretics, oxygen) and the indications for hospitalization.
  4. Provide supportive nursing care: respiratory assessment, oxygen, fluids, fever control, airway clearance with suctioning, positioning, and family education.

2. Placement Box

Ward: Medical ward — first of two medical topics. The guide reuses CH6 respiratory assessment and CH10 core ward skills (hand hygiene, standard/droplet precautions), CH7 fluid therapy, and CH8 medication safety.

4. Definition and Classification

  • Pneumonia = inflammation of the pulmonary parenchyma, common in childhood, more frequent in infancy and early childhood; may occur as a primary disease or complication of another illness.
  • The most useful classification is by etiologic agent: viral, bacterial, mycoplasmal (atypical), or aspiration. Agents are usually introduced through inhalation or from the bloodstream.
  • Pneumonitis is a localized acute inflammation without the toxemia of lobar pneumonia.
  • Common organisms by age:
  • Neonates: group B streptococci, gram-negative enteric bacteria, CMV, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Listeria, C. trachomatis.
  • Infants and preschool: RSV, parainfluenza, influenza, adenovirus, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. pneumoniae, group A streptococci.
  • School-age: M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, M. tuberculosis, group A streptococci, S. aureus, respiratory viruses.

5. Types

5.1 Viral Pneumonia

  • Occur more frequently than bacterial pneumonias; often associated with viral URIs; interstitial pneumonitis with mucosal inflammation of bronchi/bronchioles.
  • Onset acute or insidious: mild fever, slight cough, malaise → high fever, severe cough, fatigue; cough early unproductive or with small amounts of whitish sputum; radiography: diffuse or patchy infiltration with peribronchial distribution.
  • Prognosis good, but viral infection predisposes to secondary bacterial invasion (denuded bronchial mucosa). Treatment is symptomatic — oxygen, chest percussion/postural drainage, antipyretics, fluid intake; antibiotics only when bacterial infection is demonstrated.

5.2 Primary Atypical Pneumonia

  • Caused by pathogens other than the common bacteria; *M. pneumoniae* is the most common cause of community-acquired pneumonia in children 5 years or older; more prevalent in fall/winter and crowded conditions.
  • Onset sudden/insidious with systemic symptoms: fever, chills (older children), headache, malaise, anorexia, myalgia; then rhinitis, sore throat, and dry hacking cough producing seromucoid sputum that becomes mucopurulent or blood-streaked; fever may last several days to 2 weeks; dyspnea infrequent.
  • Radiography may show pneumonia before physical signs; recovery in 7–10 days with symptomatic treatment; incubation 2–3 weeks; cough may last weeks; hospitalization rarely needed; erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin are primary agents.

5.3 Bacterial Pneumonia

  • *S. pneumoniae* is the most common bacterial pathogen for community-acquired pneumonia in children and adults; others: group A streptococcus, S. aureus, M. catarrhalis, M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae.
  • Neonatal: infection within 3–5 days of birth usually from maternal organisms (transplacental or aspirated amniotic fluid); onset abrupt and nonspecific — may include respiratory distress.
  • Beyond the neonatal period, onset often follows a viral infection; in 3-month–5-year group: S. pneumoniae, M. catarrhalis, group A streptococci common; H. influenzae type B less common since Hib vaccine; S. aureus is rare but aggressively progressive.
  • The child appears ill: fever, malaise, rapid shallow respirations, cough, chest pain (may be referred to the abdomen in young children); chills and meningism common; cough may persist weeks to months.

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Fever: usually high.
  • Respiratory: cough (unproductive → productive whitish sputum); tachypnea; breath sounds crackles/rhonchi; dullness with percussion; chest pain; retractions; nasal flaring; pallor to cyanosis (severity-dependent).
  • Chest radiography: diffuse/patchy infiltration with peribronchial distribution.
  • Behavior: irritability, restlessness, lethargy.
  • GI: anorexia, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain.
  • Infants: more severe symptoms; cyanosis and apnea common; abrupt fever, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal distention; suspect bacterial pneumonia in all neonates with respiratory symptoms (high morbidity/mortality).

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Clinical history, age, health history, physical examination, radiography, and laboratory examination help identify the etiologic agent.
  • Radiograph: diffuse/patchy infiltration, peribronchial distribution; pleural effusion/empyema detection by radiograph; needle aspiration/thoracentesis if fluid is present.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • Most older children are treated at home with: antibiotic therapy, rest, liberal oral fluids, and antipyretics for fever; chest percussion/postural drainage may be indicated (limited evidence in CAP).
  • Hospitalization indications: pleural effusion or empyema; moderate/severe respiratory distress or deoxygenation; poor compliance; infants younger than 6 months; chronic illnesses (CHD, BPD); IV fluids for hydration; oxygen if in respiratory distress; some need parenteral antibiotics initially.
  • Complications: empyema, pyopneumothorax or tension pneumothorax (especially staphylococcal pneumonia in infants), AOM and pleural effusion with pneumococcal pneumonia; pneumococcal vaccination is important prevention.
  • Pleural effusion/empyema management: thoracentesis; continuous closed chest drainage with chest tube for 5–7 days; intrapleural fibrinolytics or video-assisted thoracoscopy; rare open debridement.

9. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 4-month-old, RSV-positive infant with pneumonia: RR 68/min, subcostal retractions, copious nasal secretions, refusing feeds, temperature 39 °C.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Ineffective airway clearance related to secretions.
  • Impaired gas exchange related to inflammation and airway obstruction.
  • Deficient fluid volume / risk for aspiration related to poor feeding and secretions.

Goals: Patent airway; O₂ sat within target; adequate hydration; temperature controlled; family empowered.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Frequency of assessment: monitor respiratory rate and status, oxygenation, general disposition, and activity level frequently — early detection of deterioration.
  2. Oxygenation: administer O₂ by nasal cannula, face mask, blow-by, or face tent; pulse oximetry as indicated.
  3. Positioning: semierect position (child's comfort); lying on the affected side for unilateral pneumonia ("good lung up") splints the chest and reduces pleural rubbing.
  4. Airway clearance: suction ineffectual secretions with bulb syringe/mechanical suction (especially in infants); postural drainage/percussion/nebulized bronchodilators if ordered.
  5. Fluids: IV fluids during the acute phase; NG tube for hydration/antibiotics if oral intake poor; small frequent fluids (5–10 mL per medication syringe every 10 min) for infants; offer fluids even if solids are refused.
  6. Fever control: cool environment + antipyretics as prescribed.
  7. Infection control: hand hygiene, PPE; limit crowding; droplet precautions per policy.
  8. Family education: observe for worsening symptoms; antibiotic/antipyretic administration; encourage fluids; involve the family to reduce child anxiety.
  9. Chest-tube care (if effusion): monitor drainage, vacuum, kinks, intact dressing, water seal, tube below entry site; encourage movement; analgesia for discomfort.
  10. Evaluate: RR improves, SpO₂ stable, secretions cleared, urine output adequate, fever down, parent reports concerns.

Chapter 17 — Acute Diarrhea, Vomiting, and Dehydration

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. State the common causes of acute gastroenteritis (rotavirus is the leading cause of serious gastroenteritis) and its pathophysiology (secretory vs invasive diarrhea → dehydration, acidosis, shock).
  2. Classify dehydration as mild (5–6%), moderate (7–9%), or severe (>9%) and identify the signs of each.
  3. Apply the rehydration protocol: ORS 50 mL/kg (mild) and 100 mL/kg (moderate) within 4 hours; IV lactated Ringer 40 mL/kg for severe dehydration until pulse/consciousness improve.
  4. Provide nursing care: small frequent ORS feedings even while vomiting, ongoing stool-loss replacement, I&O and daily weight monitoring, early reintroduction of diet, breastfeeding continuation, and prevention education (hand hygiene, rotavirus vaccine).

2. Placement Box

Ward: Medical ward — second medical topic. This chapter expands the fluid/electrolyte calculations from CH7 and applies the oral/IV rehydration protocol, monitoring, and communication skills from the foundation chapters.

4. Definition, Causes, and Transmission

  • Most pathogens causing diarrhea spread by the fecal-oral route (contaminated food/water) or person-to-person close contact; risk factors: lack of clean water, crowding, poor hygiene, nutritional deficiency, poor sanitation. Infants are especially susceptible because their immune systems have not acquired protective antibodies.
  • Worldwide, acute gastroenteritis is caused by viruses, bacteria, and parasites; rotavirus is the most important cause of serious gastroenteritis in children, spreads fecal-orally, and almost all children are infected at least once by age 5; it is the most common cause of diarrhea-associated hospitalization.
  • Common bacterial pathogens: Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, Shigella (raw/undercooked food or contaminated water).
  • Antibiotic-associated diarrhea: antibiotics alter intestinal flora; *C. difficile* is the most common bacterial overgrowth, usually acquired in health care facilities (20% community/daycare).

5. Pathophysiology

  • Pathogens attach to mucosal cells; diarrhea is secretory (noninvasive, toxin-producing, noninflammatory — the most common) when an enterotoxin acts after mucosal attachment, or systemic (invasive) when the organism penetrates the mucosa.
  • The most serious immediate disturbances of severe diarrheal disease: dehydration, acid-base imbalance with acidosis, and shock when dehydration seriously impairs circulation.

6. Clinical Manifestations — Assessment of Dehydration

DegreeSigns and Symptoms
Mild (5–6%)Increased thirst; slightly dry buccal mucous membranes
Moderate (7–9%)Loss of skin turgor; dry buccal mucous membranes; sunken eyes; sunken fontanel
Severe (>9%)Signs of moderate dehydration plus one of: rapid thready pulse, cyanosis, rapid breathing, lethargy, coma

If no signs of dehydration are present, rehydration therapy is not necessary — proceed with maintenance therapy and replacement of stool losses.

Additional moderate–severe findings: decreased tear production, sunken orbits, dry mucous membranes, listlessness/lethargy, skin tenting; heart rate increases in moderate dehydration but blood pressure stays normal until it decreases in severe dehydration.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Usually clinical (history of diarrhea/vomiting + examination); inspect stool color/consistency, auscultate hyperactive bowel sounds in gastroenteritis vs hypoactive in obstruction, palpate for tenderness.
  • Identify pathogens when indicated (stool culture/PCR) especially for persistent, bloody, or daycare-associated diarrhea.

8. Therapeutic Management

8.1 Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) — the mainstay

  • ORS should contain 75 mmol/L sodium chloride and 13.5 g/L glucose (standard products: Pedialyte, Infalyte); tap water, milk, undiluted fruit juice, soup, and broth are NOT appropriate for rehydration.
  • Mild dehydration: ORS 50 mL/kg within 4 hours.
  • Moderate dehydration: ORS 100 mL/kg within 4 hours; after reevaluation continue ORS if still dehydrated.
  • Vomiting is NOT a contraindication to ORS: give ORS at frequent intervals in small amounts — 5–10 mL increments (spoon or small syringe) every 2–5 minutes for young children; ORS may also be given by NG or gastrostomy tube.
  • Replacement of ongoing stool losses: ORS 10 mL/kg (infants) or 150–250 mL (older children) for each diarrheal stool; if stool volume is unknown, about 10 mL/kg is the guide.
  • Maintenance nutrition: breastfeeding continues; regular formula if tolerated (lactose-free formula for suspected lactose intolerance); children on solids continue their usual diet; early reintroduction of the normal diet lessens duration of illness and improves weight gain.
  • Dietary cautions: avoid high-carbohydrate fluids (fruit juice, Kool-Aid — low in electrolytes, decrease stool transit time); discourage milk/high-sugar fluids during the acute phase.

8.2 IV (Parenteral) Therapy

  • IV fluids are for severe dehydration, uncontrollable vomiting, inability to drink, or failure of ORT.
  • Severe dehydration: IV lactated Ringer (or normal saline) 40 mL/kg until pulse and state of consciousness return to normal, then ORS 50–100 mL/kg can be given. Initial bolus 20 mL/kg of NS or LR, then reassess hydration.
  • Parenteral rehydration proceeds in phases: initial deficit replacement, maintenance, and replacement of ongoing losses.

9. Vomiting Management

  • Vomiting is a symptom of some underlying condition; therapeutic management usually involves slow oral rehydration and possibly antiemetics.
  • Withhold oral feeding for 1–2 hours after emesis, then start ORT; give the infant/child 0.5–2 oz (about 15–60 mL) of ORS every 15 minutes depending on age and size; most retain small amounts; larger amounts as improvement occurs.
  • If oral rehydration is impossible because of continued nausea/vomiting → IV fluids; antiemetics (e.g., ondansetron — preferred over promethazine for fewer side effects) may control vomiting.
  • Bilious vomiting is the main symptom of bowel obstruction (e.g., malrotation with volvulus) — emergency.

10. Worked Scenario Examples (Calculations)

Example 1 — Moderate dehydration, ORS volume : A 10-kg toddler has moderate dehydration (7–9%).

  • ORS needed in 4 h = 100 mL/kg × 10 kg = 1000 mL over 4 hours.
  • Ongoing stool-replacement = 10 mL/kg per stool = 100 mL ORS per diarrheal stool.

Example 2 — Mild dehydration, ORS volume: A 6-kg infant with mild dehydration.

  • ORS in 4 h = 50 mL/kg × 6 kg = 300 mL over 4 hours.
  • Give 5–10 mL every 2–5 min by syringe to minimize vomiting.

Example 3 — Severe dehydration, IV bolus: A 12-kg child with severe dehydration (rapid thready pulse, lethargy).

  • IV lactated Ringer 40 mL/kg = 12 × 40 = 480 mL until pulse and consciousness normalize, then ORS 50–100 mL/kg.

Example 4 — Maintenance (review from CH7): Holliday–Segar: for 10 kg = 1000 mL/day + ongoing stool losses each replaced 1:1 with ORS (10 mL/kg per stool).

11. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 8-month-old, 3 days of watery stools (10/day), vomiting after feeds, decreased tears, dry mucous membranes, sunken fontanel, skin turgor tents; temperature 38.5 °C; input very low.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Deficient fluid volume related to diarrhea and vomiting.
  • Imbalanced nutrition: less than body requirements related to poor intake and losses.
  • Risk for impaired skin integrity (diaper area).

Goals: Rehydrated within 4 h; urine output adequate; no worsening; skin intact; parent competent in ORT.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Give ORS 100 mL/kg over 4 h in 5–10 mL increments by spoon/syringe every 2–5 min; continue despite vomiting — effective rehydration even with emesis.
  2. Replace each diarrheal stool with ORS 10 mL/kg — prevents recurrent deficit.
  3. Assess hydration and daily weight; strict I & O; assess skin turgor, oral mucosa, tears, fontanel every 4–8 h; daily weight is one of the best indicators of fluid status — early detection of failure.
  4. Monitor urine output (0.5–1 mL/kg/h adequate) — end-organ perfusion gauge.
  5. Continue breastfeeding; resume formula/usual diet early after rehydration — nutrition, lessens illness duration.
  6. Skin care: barrier ointment in the diaper area; frequent diaper changes — prevents excoriation.
  7. IV fluids if severe dehydration, uncontrollable vomiting, or failed ORT; initial bolus 20 mL/kg NS/LR then reassess — restores circulation.
  8. Prevention education: hand hygiene; rotavirus vaccination (first dose by 15 weeks of age); avoid sharing cups/utensils — breaks the fecal-oral cycle.
  9. Evaluate: 4-h weight gain, urine output adequate, tolerates feeding, parent demonstrates ORS technique.

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  2. Recognize the clinical manifestations in the newborn (delayed meconium, bilious vomiting, distention), infancy (FTT, constipation, enterocolitis), and childhood (ribbonlike stools, palpable fecal mass).

2. Placement Box

Ward: Surgical ward — first surgical topic. This chapter applies the surgical-preparation and procedure skills (CH20) plus core ward skills (CH10), fluid monitoring (CH7), and post-op pain/nutrition care.

4. Definition and Epidemiology

  • Hirschsprung disease (HD) is a congenital anomaly resulting in mechanical obstruction from inadequate motility of part of the intestine. It accounts for about one-fourth of all neonatal intestinal obstructions; incidence 1 in 5,000 live births, four times more common in males, small familial pattern (RET proto-oncogene mutations).

5. Pathophysiology

  • The pathology relates to the absence of ganglion cells in the affected intestine (in the myenteric plexus of Auerbach and submucosal plexus of Meissner), with loss of the rectosphincteric reflex.
  • Absence of enteric nervous system stimulation decreases the internal sphincter's ability to relax; unopposed sympathetic stimulation increases intestinal tone; peristalsis is lost and the internal sphincter does not relax when a stool bolus enters the rectum.
  • In about 80% of cases the aganglionic segment is confined to the rectum and part of the distal colon (short-segment disease); the whole colon or part of the small intestine can be involved (long-segment, ~20%); total intestinal aganglionosis in ~5%.

6. Clinical Manifestations

Newborn period:

  • Failure to pass meconium within 24–48 hours after birth; refusal to feed; bilious vomiting; abdominal distention.

Infancy:

  • Failure to thrive; constipation; abdominal distention; episodes of diarrhea and vomiting.
  • Signs of enterocolitis: explosive, watery diarrhea; fever; appears significantly ill.

Childhood:

  • Constipation; ribbonlike, foul-smelling stools; abdominal distention; visible peristalsis; easily palpable fecal mass; undernourished, anemic appearance.

Note: 99% of term infants pass meconium within the first 48 hours, whereas few infants with HD do so.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Suspected in the neonate from signs of obstruction or failure to pass meconium; in infants/children, the history of a chronic pattern of constipation; on examination the rectum is empty of feces, the internal sphincter is tight, and leakage of liquid stool/gas may occur in short-segment disease.
  • Contrast enema often demonstrates the transition zone between the dilated proximal colon (megacolon) and the aganglionic distal segment (may not develop until age 2 months).
  • Rectal biopsy (surgical full-thickness or suction biopsy) confirms the diagnosis by demonstrating the absence of ganglion cells.
  • Anorectal manometry records the sphincter reflex response to balloon distention: in HD the internal sphincter fails to relax while the external sphincter contracts normally.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • The majority require surgery; one of three procedures: the Swenson (pull-through), the Duhamel procedure, and the Soave (endorectal pull-through).
  • Surgery is performed after the child is stabilized with fluid and electrolyte replacement and colonic cleansing with enemas; the transanal Soave endorectal pull-through pulls normal bowel through the rectal muscular sleeve from which aganglionic mucosa has been removed; earlier diagnosis allows a primary one-stage procedure without a temporary colostomy.
  • After pull-through, most children achieve fecal continence; possible complications: anal stricture, recurrent enterocolitis, prolapse, perianal abscess, incontinence — needing dilations or bowel-retraining therapy.

9. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 3-day-old male, failed to pass meconium, bilious vomiting, distended abdomen, refusing feeds, temperature unstable.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Impaired elimination / risk for constipation related to aganglionosis.
  • Risk for deficient fluid volume related to vomiting and withheld feedings.
  • Risk for infection (enterocolitis, perforation) after surgery.
  • Deficient knowledge (parents) related to home care.

Goals: Meconium/stool passage achieved or colostomy functioning; no enterocolitis; abdomen decompressed; parents competent in home care.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Preoperative stabilization: monitor vitals/BP for signs of shock; monitor fluid/electrolyte replacement and plasma/blood derivatives; give enemas, oral/systemic antibiotics, and colonic irrigations per protocol — prevents toxicity and prepares the bowel.
  2. Observe for perforation: fever, increasing abdominal distention, vomiting, increased tenderness, irritability, dyspnea, cyanosis — early detection.
  3. Measure abdominal circumference serially at a marked point (e.g., umbilicus/widest part) with paper tape; leave the tape in place for the acutely ill child — progressive distention is serious.
  4. Feeding/fluids: NPO with IV fluids before surgery; resume feeds postoperatively as ordered and involve parents in feedings.
  5. Postoperative care as for any abdominal surgery: wound observation, analgesia, early ambulation per order, inspect for wound infection and irregular passage of stool.
  6. Teach parents: signs of enterocolitis, fecal incontinence, and obstruction; daily anal dilations to avoid anastomotic strictures (parents often perform at home with encouragement and detailed instructions); colostomy care with meticulous skin care when a diverting colostomy is used.
  7. Support the parents adjusting to a congenital defect; foster infant–parent bonding.
  8. Evaluate: stable vitals, decreasing abdominal girth, stool/colostomy output present, no enterocolitis; parents demonstrate dilation/colostomy care.

2. Placement Box

Ward: Surgical ward — second surgical topic and the final ministerial chapter. HPS brilliantly ties together vomiting management (CH17) and fluid/electrolyte monitoring (CH7) with surgical care (CH20).

4. Definition and Epidemiology

  • Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (HPS) occurs when the circumferential muscle of the pyloric sphincter thickens, elongating and narrowing the pyloric canal → gastric outlet obstruction with compensatory dilation, hypertrophy, and hyperperistalsis of the stomach.
  • Develops in the first few weeks of life, causing nonbilious vomiting after feeding; projectile vomiting may develop, and the infant is fussy and hungry after vomiting; untreated → dehydration, metabolic alkalosis, and failure to thrive.
  • Boys are affected 4–6 times more often than girls; more common in white infants; incidence 2–3.5 per 1,000 live births, with 30–40% first-born infants; etiology multifactorial.
  • HPS is not a congenital disorder; local innervation may be involved; usually an isolated lesion, but may associate with malrotation, esophageal/duodenal atresia, and anorectal anomalies.

5. Pathophysiology

  • The circular pyloric muscle thickens → severe narrowing of the pyloric canal between stomach and duodenum; the lumen is partially obstructed, and over time inflammation and edema further reduce the opening → complete obstruction.
  • The hypertrophied pylorus is palpable as an olive-like mass in the upper abdomen.

6. Clinical Manifestations

  • Projectile vomiting: may be ejected 3–4 feet when side-lying, 1 foot or more when back-lying; usually shortly after a feeding; may follow each feeding or appear intermittently; nonbilious vomitus that may be blood tinged.
  • Infant hungry, avid nurser — eagerly accepts a second feeding after vomiting; no evidence of pain except chronic hunger; poor weight gain; signs of dehydration.
  • Distended upper abdomen; readily palpable olive-shaped tumor in the epigastrium just to the right of the umbilicus; visible gastric peristaltic waves moving left to right across the epigastrium.
  • Vomiting usually begins at 4 weeks of age (range 1 week to 5 months), occurring 10–30 minutes after feeding and becoming projectile as obstruction progresses.
  • Mild indirect hyperbilirubinemia (decreased glucuronyl transferase) may occur and resolves with surgical correction.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Diagnosis is often made after history and physical examination; the olive is most easily palpated when the stomach is empty, the infant is quiet, and abdominal muscles relaxed.
  • If inconclusive: ultrasonography demonstrates an elongated mass surrounding a long pyloric canal; if US is negative, upper GI radiography rules out other causes.
  • A pacifier or nipple dipped in glucose water may soothe the irritable infant enough to complete the abdominal examination.
  • Laboratory findings (dehydration): decreased serum sodium and potassium (may be masked by hemoconcentration); more diagnostically useful — decrease in serum chloride and increases in pH and bicarbonate (metabolic alkalosis); elevated BUN from dehydration.

8. Therapeutic Management

  • Surgical relief by pyloromyotomy is the standard therapy. Preoperatively the infant must be rehydrated and the metabolic alkalosis corrected with parenteral fluids and electrolytes; replacement therapy usually delays surgery 24–48 hours; stomach decompressed with an NG tube if vomiting continues; infants without imbalance go to surgery without delay.
  • Surgery (often laparoscopic): longitudinal incision through the circular muscle fibers down to, but not including, the submucosa — pyloromyotomy (Fredet–Ramstedt operation); high success rate.
  • Feedings: usually begun 4–6 hours postoperatively with small, frequent feedings of water or electrolyte solution; if retained, formula in small increments about 24 hours after surgery; gradually increase until full schedule (~48 hours).
  • Prognosis: excellent with early diagnosis; mortality 0–0.5%; small percentage develop GERD.

9. Nursing Care Plan

Assessment: 5-week-old male, 1-week history of projectile nonbilious vomiting after feeds, hungry between episodes, weight falling (birth 3.6 kg → now 3.4 kg), signs of dehydration.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Deficient fluid volume related to vomiting.
  • Imbalanced nutrition: less than body requirements related to gastric outlet obstruction.
  • Risk for aspiration / ineffective airway related to projectile vomiting.
  • Deficient knowledge (parents) related to the disorder and post-op care.

Goals: Rehydrated and alkalosis corrected preoperatively; successful pyloromyotomy; staged feeds tolerated; parents confident and reassured.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Preoperative care — restore hydration/electrolytes: NPO, IV fluids with glucose and electrolytes based on serum values; careful monitoring of IV fluids and strict I & O; accurate description of vomiting and stools — corrects alkalosis before surgery.
  2. Monitor electrolytes: vitals especially for fluid/electrolyte imbalance; metabolic alkalosis from loss of H⁺ and depletion of K, Na, Cl in gastric secretions; assess skin/mucous membranes.
  3. NG decompression: keep the tube patent and functioning; measure/record type and amount of drainage if used preoperatively.
  4. Assist in diagnosis: observe feeding behavior, peristaltic waves, and palpation of the olive (empty stomach, quiet, glucose-water pacifier).
  5. Reassure parents: the condition is a structural problem, not poor parenting; encourage visits and involvement in care — reduces anxiety.
  6. Postoperative feeding: start 12–24 h (water/electrolytes 4–6 h per protocol), small frequent amounts; give expressed breast milk or limit nursing time with gradual increase; record feedings and responses.
  7. Postoperative observation: expect some vomiting in the first 24–48 h; monitor vitals, IV fluids, I&O; give around-the-clock analgesics (pain is continuous); inspect incisions for drainage/erythema and report infection signs.
  8. Discharge teaching: incision/dressing care, feeding schedule and expectations, when to call the surgeon.
  9. Evaluate: urine output adequate, alkalosis corrected, feedings retained without projectile vomiting, parents verbalize confidence.

Procedure 20.05 — Blood Pressure Measurement (Correct Cuff, Child Technique)

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Vital-sign skill · Time: ≈3–5 min (after 5 min of quiet) · Applies to: all ages · Chapter 20 — Core Clinical Procedures

Purpose — Why we do this procedure

  • Measure blood pressure accurately in children using the correct cuff size.
  • Cuff size is the single most important determinant of a reliable reading.

Assessment — What to check before you start

  • Upper-arm circumference to select the cuff.
  • Child state: seated or supine, calm and quiet for 5 minutes before measurement.
  • Previous readings, age percentiles, and medications.

Equipment — What you need

  • Age-appropriate cuff (neonatal, infant, child, small-adult sizes), manual sphygmomanometer or validated oscillometric device.

Procedure Steps

#ActionRationale / precision pointDone
1Select a cuff whose bladder width ≈ 40% of the arm circumference; confirm the bladder encircles the limb.A cuff too small overestimates and one too large underestimates BP.☐
2Seat the child with the arm supported at heart level (newborn: supine).Arm position at heart level gives the correct hydrostatic reference.☐
3Place the cuff 2–3 cm above the antecubital fossa over the brachial artery; ensure a snug fit.Correct placement over the artery gives a reliable reading.☐

7Record BP, site, cuff size, and position used.Reproducibility depends on documenting the method.☐

  • Correct cuff width ≈40% of the upper-arm circumference (bladder encircles 80–100% of the arm).

  • An isolated elevated reading may reflect stress, not hypertension — recheck with correct technique.

Safety alerts — Protect the child

  • A cuff that is too small reads falsely high — always measure the arm circumference first.
  • Do not take BP in an arm with an IV, infusion, or recent trauma.
  • For oscillometric devices, still use the correct cuff size; an arm cuff used on the thigh needs a larger cuff.

Documentation — What to record

  • BP with cuff size, site, and child's position on the vital-sign flow sheet.

  • Student selects the correct cuff by measurement and obtains a reading at heart level.

Procedure 20.13 — SC, IM, and ID Injections plus Drug-Dose Calculation

Clinical Pediatric Nursing · for Ward and Laboratory Training

Medication administration · Time: ≈10 min · Applies to: all ages (site by age) · Chapter 20 — Core Clinical Procedures

Purpose — Why we do this procedure

  • Administer medications safely by subcutaneous (SC), intramuscular (IM), and intradermal (ID) routes in children.
  • Verify that every dose is calculated and checked before administration.

Assessment — What to check before you start

  • The 8 rights: right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, assessment, right to refuse/education.

  • Site by age: vastus lateralis preferred <3 years; deltoid after ≈15 months with enough muscle; dorsogluteal avoided in children.

Equipment — What you need

  • Correct syringe (1 mL tuberculin for ID/subunit vaccines; 2–3 mL IM), correct-gauge needle (IM 22–25 G; SC 25–26 G; ID 26–27 G), alcohol swab, gloves, sharps container, drug/vial and the original order.

Procedure Steps

#ActionRationale / precision pointDone
1Calculate the dose: ordered mg ÷ available mg/mL = volume (mL); recheck against the mg/kg recommendation and have it double-checked.Volume (mL) = ordered dose (mg) ÷ drug concentration (mg/mL) — arithmetic errors are the main cause of drug errors.☐
2Verify the 8 rights using the original order + physician/doc read-back; check expiry and appearance.Independent verification prevents wrong drug/dose/patient errors.☐
3Prepare and draw the drug under aseptic technique; expel air; cap the needle.Asepsis and air expulsion maintain sterility and correct dose.☐
4Select and palpate the correct site for the age (vastus lateralis for infants; deltoid >3 yr; inner forearm for ID).Age-appropriate sites avoid nerves and vessels.☐

8ID: stretch the skin; insert at 5–15° bevel-up; inject a small bleb/wheal; do not massage or wipe (Mantoux: mark/read on schedule).ID injection stays in the dermis; a wheal confirms technique.☐
9Dispose of the sharps immediately in the puncture-resistant container; never recap two-handed.Immediate disposal prevents needle-stick injury.☐
10Document the drug, dose, route, site, time, and the child's response; observe for local/systemic reaction.Documentation completes the medication cycle and tracks effects.☐
IM injection at the vastus lateralis vs deltoid; ID injection angle

Normal values — Benchmarks for this skill

  • Volume limits: IM ≤2 mL in a single site (older children); ≤1 mL in infants/smaller children; SC up to 1 mL; ID small (≤0.1 mL).
  • Angles: IM 90°; SC 45–90°; ID 5–15°.
  • Dose formula: Volume (mL) = ordered dose (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL) [CH7, CH8].

Safety alerts — Protect the child

  • Two-nurse dose double-check for high-alert drugs.
  • Do not aspirate for vaccines (current vaccine guidance [CH09]).
  • Dorsogluteal site is avoided in children (sciatic-nerve risk, poor muscle development).

  • Drug, dose (mg and mL), route, site, time, and signature.

  • Student calculates a weight-based dose correctly, selects the correct IM/SC/ID site and angle for the age, and performs the injection on a manikin with correct technique.

4. Writing SMART Goals

  • Specific — "Pass urine at least 1 mL/kg/h" (not "better hydration").
  • Measurable — use numbers (urine output, weight, temperature, SaO₂, pain scale).
  • Achievable, Relevant to the diagnosis, Time-bound ("within 8 h", "by discharge").

Example (fever in pneumonia): goal — "Body temperature within the age normal range (<38 °C axillary) within 12 h of starting antipyretics and fluids." Interventions: antipyretic per order; sponge bath only if very high and per protocol; observe for shivering; increase oral fluids; recheck temperature q2–4 h.

5. Care-Plan Template (use in ward assignments)

  2. Problem list (from assessment): data + nursing diagnosis.
  3. Goals (SMART): short-term + long-term.
  4. Interventions with rationale: for each, state what you do and why (cite the chapter source).
  5. Evaluation: outcome measures + revision.

6. Case-Study Practice

Give the care plan for: a 4-month-old with bronchiolitis/pneumonia (fever 39 °C, RR 58/min, SpO₂ 90% on room air, feeding poorly).
