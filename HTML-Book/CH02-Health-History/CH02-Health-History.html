1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. State the parts of the pediatric health history (identifying information, CC, HPI, PMH, ROS, family history, psychosocial history).
  2. Elicit the chief complaint with open-ended, neutral questions.
  3. Take the history of present illness as a narrative from onset to present (onset, interval, present status, reason for seeking help now).
  4. Take the birth history (mother's health during pregnancy, labor and delivery, infant condition after birth) and explain its relevance to parents.
  5. Ask the past medical history (illnesses, injuries, surgeries, allergies, current medications, immunizations, growth and development).
  6. Adapt the history depth to the child's age and to emergent vs well-child situations.
  7. Perform the lab and hospital steps of the history-taking interview using the chapter checklist.

2. Placement Box

4. Introduction: The Pediatric Health History

The interview is a specific form of goal-directed communication. In the child, the health history is taken from the parent(s) or caregivers, with input from the child when old enough to speak for themselves. An organized approach is most effective.

The complete pediatric health history is outlined in Box 4.4 , which includes:

  1. Identifying information — name, address, telephone, birth date and place, race/ethnic group, sex, religion, date of interview, informant.
  2. Chief complaint (CC) — the major specific reason for seeking health care.
  3. History of present illness (HPI) — all details related to the CC.
  4. Past medical history (PMH) — birth history, previous illnesses/injuries/surgeries, allergies, current medications, immunizations, growth and development, habits.
  5. Review of systems (ROS) — each body system screened.
  6. Family history — relevant hereditary or familial conditions.
  7. Psychosocial history — family structure, habits, school, environment.
  8. Dietary/nutritional history — detailed for the child.

5. The Chief Complaint (CC)

  • The chief complaint is the specific reason for the child's visit; it may be the theme, with the present illness viewed as the description of the problem.
  • Elicit the CC with open-ended, neutral questions: "What seems to be the matter?", "How may I help you?", "Why did you come here today?".
  • Do not use labeling-type questions ("How are you sick?", "What is the problem?") — they limit information.
  • The reason for the visit may not be an illness — it may be a developmental concern, school referral, or procedure.

6. History of Present Illness (HPI)

The HPI is a narrative of the chief complaint from its onset through its progression to the present. Four major components:

  1. Details of onset
  2. A complete interval history
  3. The present status
  4. The reason for seeking help now

The focus is on all factors relevant to the main problem, even if they have disappeared or changed.

Analyzing a symptom

  • Type (character)
  • Location
  • Severity — best determined by how it affects the child's usual behavior; pain that prevents playing, interacting, sleeping, and eating is most often severe; use a rating scale such as the numeric or Wong-Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale
  • Duration — onset, frequency, described in terms of activity/behavior
  • Influencing factors — precipitating, relieving, temporal, positional, and associated events

7. Past Medical History (PMH)

7.1 Birth history

Includes all data concerning:

  1. The mother's health during pregnancy
  2. The labor and delivery
  3. The infant's condition immediately after birth

Because prenatal influences significantly affect a child's physical and emotional development, a thorough birth history is essential. Explain its relevance when parents question it: "I will be asking you some questions about your pregnancy and [child's name]'s birth. Your answers will give me a more complete picture of his overall health".

7.2 Previous illnesses, injuries, and surgeries

  • Begin with a general question ("What other illnesses has your child had?"), then specifically ask about colds, earaches, measles, rubella, chickenpox, mumps, pertussis, diphtheria, tuberculosis, scarlet fever, strep throat, recurrent ear infections, influenza, gastroesophageal reflux, tonsillitis, and allergic manifestations.
  • Ask about injuries that required intervention (falls, poisoning, choking, concussion, fractures, burns), surgeries, procedures, and hospitalizations with dates and diagnoses.

7.3 Allergies

Ask about hay fever, asthma, unusual drug/food/latex reactions, and contact agents. Ask specifically and reliably about drug reactions : name of the drug, the reaction, route, time to reaction, recurrence on rechallenge, and whether it was documented.

Nursing Alert: information about allergic reactions is essential — failure to document a serious reaction places the child at risk if the agent is given.

7.4 Current medications

Ask about vitamins, antipyretics (especially aspirin), antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, supplements, herbs/essential oils, homeopathic medicines. Record name, dose, schedule, duration, reason. Ask parents to bring the containers or call the pharmacy (OTC drugs won't appear on prescriptions).

7.5 Immunizations

A record of all immunizations is essential; list the disease, number of injections, dose, date, and any reaction. Sources: provider record, school record, centralized immunization registry. Screening for contraindications and precautions before every vaccine.

7.6 Growth and development

  • Growth: measurements of height/length and head circumference at birth, patterns on the growth chart, deviations from previous percentiles, family concerns.
  • Development: age of head control, sitting alone, walking, first words, bladder/bowel control, school grade and performance, best friend, interactions with peers and adults. Use specific, detailed questions ("sitting up" can mean different things).

8. Adapting the History to the Child's Age and Situation

  • Infants: emphasize the birth history, detailed feeding history, immunizations, and growth and development.
  • Older children and adolescents: history taking should include the child directly; give older children the opportunity to talk without the parents present.
  • Emergent situations: a focused health history — chief complaint and essentials only.

9. Nursing Care Plan (History/Interview Focus)

Assessment: parent anxious, short answers; child plays in background.

NANDA diagnosis: Risk for compromised family coping / Anxiety (parent) related to the health concern.

Goals: The parent will provide a complete, accurate history; anxiety cues will decrease during the interview.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Establish the setting (introduction, privacy, preferred names) before questions — builds trust and reliability.
  2. Begin each section with an open-ended statement ("Tell me about your child's birth") then use fact-finding questions to focus — yields the most complete data.
  3. Explain why birth-history questions are asked for an older child — increases cooperation.
  4. Use the medication containers and the allergy checklist — improves accuracy.
  5. Evaluate: history complete; parent reports feeling heard; no vital data omitted.

10. Case Study (History led to diagnosis)

Hiba, 6 weeks. The mother brought her to the clinic because "she vomits after every feed." CC confirmed with an open-ended question. HPI: projectile vomiting began at 4 weeks, worsening; the mother states the baby is hungry again soon after feeding. PMH birth history: full term, normal delivery, birth weight 3.2 kg.

  • Which CC question was most appropriate? (A neutral open-ended: "What seems to be the matter?")
  • Which part of the HPI is now incomplete? (Analyzing the symptom: type, severity, duration, influencing factors — here the feeding–vomiting relationship)
  • Why does the nurse explain the birth-history questions? (To improve cooperation)

This history pattern (projectile, non-bilious, hungry after feeds) is the classic presentation re-trained in Chapter 19 (Pyloric stenosis).

12. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Repeat the checklist at the bedside, using the ward's admission form.
  • Always record the informant and the reliability of the information.
  • Document allergies on the allergy/medication record (red alert).
  • Never perform an incomplete history "because the ward is busy" — the full outline protects the child.

14. Key Points

  • The pediatric history follows Box 4.4 : identifying info, CC, HPI, PMH (birth/allergies/meds/immunizations/growth), ROS, family, psychosocial, dietary.
  • Elicit the CC with open-ended, neutral questions; never labeling questions.
  • The HPI is a narrative with four components: onset, interval history, present status, and reason for seeking help now.
  • The birth history covers pregnancy, labor/delivery, and neonatal condition — explain its relevance when challenged.
  • Document allergies reliably — failure to document puts the child at risk.
  • Adapt depth: infants need birth/feeding/immunization/growth emphasis; emergencies get a focused history.
