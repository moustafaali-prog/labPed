1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student will be able to:

  1. State the epidemiology of ALL (the most common childhood cancer; peak age 3–5 years; more common in boys) and the main risk factors.
  2. Link the clinical signs (anemia, infection/neutropenia, bleeding, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, fever) to bone-marrow infiltration.
  3. Describe the diagnostic work-up (CBC, peripheral smear, bone marrow aspiration with blast percentage, CSF for CNS evaluation) and the three treatment phases (induction, consolidation/intensification, maintenance).
  4. Explain nursing care: prevention of infection, bleeding and tumor lysis syndrome, CNS-directed intrathecal therapy, mouth care, and family education.

2. Placement Box

4. Definition and Epidemiology

  • Leukemia is a broad term for malignant diseases of the bone marrow, blood, and lymphatic system. In healthy children the marrow makes blood stem cells that mature into lymphoid or myeloid cells; in acute leukemias, immature nonfunctional cells predominate.
  • The two most common types in children are acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML).
  • ALL is the most common form of childhood cancer, ~3,000 new cases annually in the U.S.; more common in boys (10% more), more common in Hispanics; peak age of onset between 3 and 5 years.
  • Risk factors: prenatal exposure to x-rays, previous treatment with chemotherapy, and genetic conditions (Down, Bloom, and Fanconi syndromes; Li-Fraumeni); some ALL begins prenatally.

5. Pathophysiology → Clinical Manifestations

  • Signs reflect infiltration of the bone marrow by nonfunctional leukemic cells (blasts). The three main consequences of marrow infiltration:
  1. Anemia from decreased RBCs;
  2. Infection from neutropenia;
  3. Bleeding from decreased platelets.
  • About half of patients have an elevated WBC count at presentation (>20,000/mm³).
  • Common signs: hepatosplenomegaly (75%), splenomegaly alone (24%), fever (63%), lymphadenopathy (60%), bleeding (petechiae/purpura, 20%), bone pain (12%).
  • Onset ranges from acute to insidious; a "cold that will not go away," pallor, listlessness, irritability, fever, anorexia, weight loss, bruising without cause, and continued bone/joint pain should arouse suspicion.
  • Two important extramedullary sanctuaries — sites where leukemic cells hide from systemic therapy: the CNS and the testes; they require specific therapy.

6. Diagnostic Evaluation

  • Suspected from history, physical signs, and a peripheral blood smear containing leukemic blasts, frequently with low counts.
  • Work-up: thorough history and physical exam; laboratory tests (CBC with differential, blood chemistries); bone marrow aspirate or biopsy with cytogenetic analysis and immunophenotyping.
  • A chest x-ray may detect a mediastinal mass.
  • Definitive diagnosis is based on analysis of the bone marrow sample — typically a monotonous infiltrate of blast cells.
  • After confirmation, a lumbar puncture checks for CNS involvement.
  • Prognostic (risk) factors including age at diagnosis (favorable 1–10 years), WBC count at diagnosis (favorable ≤50,000/mm³), CNS/testicular involvement, Down syndrome, sex, cytogenetics, and response to initial treatment (minimal residual disease, MRD).
  • NURSING ALERT: children vaccinated 2 weeks before or during chemotherapy are considered unimmunized; revaccinate or give live-virus vaccines only 6 months after chemotherapy stops, following institutional guidelines.

7. Therapeutic Management

  • Long-term disease-free survival now approaches 90% at major centers; treatment is risk-based (high-risk children get the most intensive therapy).
  • Three phases:
  1. Remission induction — begins almost immediately after diagnosis, lasts 4–6 weeks. Drugs: dexamethasone or prednisone, vincristine, L-asparaginase, with or without an anthracycline. Complete remission (CR) = <5% blasts in the marrow and no detectable extramedullary leukemia.
  2. Consolidation/intensification — given after CR (all children need post-induction therapy); the very intensive Berlin–Frankfurt–Münster (BFM) backbone uses cyclophosphamide, cytarabine, mercaptopurine, then intermittent maintenance with high-dose methotrexate (with or without leucovorin rescue) and delayed intensification.
  3. Maintenance (continuation) — daily mercaptopurine, weekly low-dose methotrexate, plus vincristine/corticosteroid and continued intrathecal therapy; adherence <90% increases relapse risk.
  • CNS-directed therapy — because leukemic cells in the CNS are protected from systemic drugs by the blood–brain barrier, all children receive CNS prophylaxis: intrathecal chemotherapy (methotrexate alone or with cytarabine + hydrocortisone) plus CNS-directed systemic therapy (dexamethasone, high-dose methotrexate with leucovorin rescue); cranial radiation for the highest-risk children.
  • Four standard treatment modalities: chemotherapy, radiation therapy, chemotherapy with stem cell transplant, and targeted therapy (e.g., TKIs, monoclonal antibodies, proteasome inhibitors).
  • Supportive therapy (essential in the period immediately after remission when myelosuppression leaves the body defenseless): transfusion support and antibacterial/antifungal agents.
  • Late effects & survivorship: long-term follow-up per the Children's Oncology Group (COG) guidelines; nurses help families access survivorship and transition care.

8. Nursing Care Plan (supportive care)

Assessment: 7-year-old boy, newly diagnosed ALL, WBC 85,000/mm³, Hgb 6.0 g/dL, platelets 60,000/mm³; hepatomegaly; starting induction tomorrow.

NANDA diagnoses:

  • Risk for infection related to neutropenia and immunosuppressive therapy.
  • Risk for injury / bleeding related to thrombocytopenia.
  • Risk for imbalanced fluid volume related to tumor lysis syndrome.

Goals: No severe infection; no significant bleeding; no tumor lysis; family educated.

Interventions (with rationale):

  1. Neutropenic precautions: hand washing, frequent bathing, no shared utensils, avoid crowds/ill contacts; monitor for fever, chills, cough, sore throat and report immediately — prevents/early-detects infection.
  2. Skin and mouth care: keep skin/perianal area clean with mild lotion; frequent mouth care with saline rinses; check for ulcers and gum swelling — prevents mucositis and skin entry of organisms.
  3. Bleeding precautions: recognize and report bruising/petechiae/nosebleeds; avoid contact sports; use soft toothbrush; monitor counts — prevents serious hemorrhage.
  4. Tumor lysis syndrome prevention: aggressive IV fluids, monitor serum chemistries frequently, check urine pH with each void, administer a drug to reduce uric acid formation, maintain strict I & O, and monitor WBC count — prevents metabolic catastrophe with high blast counts.
  5. CNS prophylaxis support: assist with lumbar puncture; obtain consent, monitor vitals, provide comfort/sedation per protocol, watch for bleeding at the puncture site.
  6. Prep for procedures: explain each diagnostic/therapeutic procedure (bone marrow aspirate, LP), what will happen, and how the child can help; assess the child's understanding — reduces fear and fosters trust.
  7. Diet: high-protein, high-calorie diet; adequate hydration with high-fiber diet and stool softeners — supports nutrition and prevents constipation.
  8. Evaluate: afebrile; no infection/bleeding; chemistry normal; family lists fever-rule and precautions.

10. Procedure — Hospital Step

  • Admit and complete the diagnostic work-up (CBC with differential, chemistries, bone marrow, LP) with family preparation and consent.
  • Begin tumor-lysis precautions the day before induction; monitor counts and chemistries throughout the day.
  • Provide CNS-intrathecal chemotherapy support in the treatment room; afterwards keep the child supine as ordered and observe the puncture site.
  • Discharge teaching: infection recognition (fever/chills/cough/sore throat → report immediately), bleeding precautions, mouth care, hydration, high-protein high-calorie diet, and vaccination advice (no live vaccines until 6 months after stopping chemo).

11. Case Study (New Diagnosis)

Scenario: A 7-year-old boy had bilateral neck swelling for 1 month; now hoarse and sleeping most of the time. CBC: Hgb 6.0 g/dL, WBC 85,000/mm³, platelets 60,000/mm³; bilateral parotid, submental and axillary lymph nodes; hepatomegaly 6 cm. Bone marrow flow cytometry: 70% B-cell blasts — ALL confirmed. Induction chemotherapy starts tomorrow.

  1. Which signs represent anemia, infection-risk, bleeding-risk, and infiltration? (Anemia → pallor/fatigue; neutropenia → infection; thrombocytopenia → bleeding/bruising; infiltration/marrow pressure → bone pain, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy.)
  2. Why is he at high risk for tumor lysis syndrome? (WBC 85,000/mm³ — massive, rapid lysis of blast cells releases intracellular contents causing metabolic abnormalities; prevent with IV fluids, uric-acid drug, urine pH checks, strict I&O.)
  3. Why intrathecal chemotherapy now? (CNS is a sanctuary where leukemic cells hide behind the blood–brain barrier; all children get CNS prophylaxis with intrathecal methotrexate-containing regimens.)
  4. What discharge teaching is essential? (Fever/immediate infection reporting, bleeding precautions, mouth and skin care, high-protein high-calorie diet, live-vaccine timing (≥6 months after chemo).)

13. Key Points

  • ALL = most common childhood cancer; peak 3–5 years, more in boys; marrow infiltration → anemia, neutropenia/infection, thrombocytopenia/bleeding, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, fever.
  • Diagnosis: CBC + peripheral smear with blasts, bone marrow aspirate (definitive), LP for CNS staging; risk-based on age, WBC, CNS status, cytogenetics, MRD.
  • Treatment: induction → consolidation/intensification → maintenance; CNS prophylaxis for all; targeted therapy and HSCT in selected/relapsed cases; supportive transfusions + anti-infectives.
  • Nursing: infection/bleeding precautions, mouth and skin care, tumor-lysis prevention (IV fluids, urine pH, uric-acid drug, I&O, chemistries), LP support, family education and survivorship.
